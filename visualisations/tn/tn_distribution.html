    The tangent-normal distribution

    A normal distribution on the tangent plane of the sphere, carried back to the sphere and to the triangle of compositions. Every picture drawn for the Kent distribution, redrawn for its tangent-normal twin.

    • \(\bmu\) — mean direction
    • \(\bgamma_2\) — first axis of \(\bK\)
    • \(\bgamma_3\) — second axis of \(\bK\)
    • \(T_{\bmu}\) — tangent plane
    • outside \(\mathcal O\) — no composition

Notation follows the manuscript: \(k\) parts, the simplex \(\mathbf S^k\), the sphere \(S^{k-1}\). All pictures use \(k=3\).

    Part 1

    Compositional data in the triangle

    A composition records how a whole splits into parts. Only the proportions matter, so the parts are scaled to sum to one.

    With three parts, a composition is \(\bz=(z_1,z_2,z_3)\) with \(z_j\ge0\) and \(z_1+z_2+z_3=1\), the closure (1) with \(c=1\). All such vectors form the simplex \(\mathbf S^3\) of (2), which for three parts is a triangle. Each corner is a composition made of a single part, each edge is where one part is zero, and the grid lines mark the levels \(z_j=0.1,0.2,\dots,0.9\).

    The 150 points below are simulated. They are drawn from the tangent-normal distribution of Part 4, so that one data set travels through the whole page.

    Part 2

    The simplex as a plane in 3D

    The triangle is not only a diagram. It is a piece of a plane in ordinary three-dimensional space.

    The condition \(z_1+z_2+z_3=1\) defines a plane in \(\R^3\), and the three conditions \(z_j\ge0\) cut a triangle out of it. Its corners are the unit vectors \(\be_1=(1,0,0)\), \(\be_2=(0,1,0)\) and \(\be_3=(0,0,1)\) on the coordinate axes. Every point of the ternary diagram is a point of this triangle. Rotate the picture, or press look along (1,1,1), and the triangle becomes the diagram of Figure 1.

    Part 3

    Projection onto the sphere

    The square root turns a composition into a unit vector, and the triangle into a curved triangle on the sphere.

    The square-root transformation of Section 2.1 takes square roots part by part, \(\bu=\sqrt{\bz}\), that is \(u_j=\sqrt{z_j}\). Because \(\sum_j z_j=1\), we get \(\sum_j u_j^2=1\): every \(\bu\) is a unit vector, a point of the sphere \(S^{k-1}=\{\bu\in\R^k:\|\bu\|=1\}\). Taking the non-negative root puts it in the positive orthant

    \[ \mathcal O=\{\bu\in S^{k-1}:\ u_j\ge0\ \text{for all } j\}, \]

    the curved triangle in the picture. The map is one-to-one, and squaring undoes it: \(\bz=\bu^2\). Zeros cause no difficulty, because \(\sqrt0=0\): a composition with a zero part lands on an edge of \(\mathcal O\), with no replacement or perturbation, unlike the log-ratio transformations.

    The square root is not the straight-line projection \(\bz/\|\bz\|\) from the origin. It stretches small parts (\(\sqrt{0.01}=0.1\)), so the projection lines below are not aimed at the origin, and the equally spaced grid lines of the triangle land unequally spaced on the sphere.

    Part 4

    The tangent-normal distribution

    The idea of Section 3.2 of the manuscript: choose a centre on the sphere, flatten the sphere around it onto the plane that touches it there, put an ordinary normal distribution on that plane, and carry it back.

    The centre and its tangent plane

    The mean direction \(\bmu\in S^{k-1}\) is a unit vector; for compositions it lies in \(\mathcal O\), and \(\bmu^2\) is the composition at the centre. Two sets belong to it, the open hemisphere centred at \(\bmu\) and the open unit ball:

    \[ H_{\bmu}=\{\bu\in S^{k-1}:\ \bmu^{\top}\bu>0\},\qquad \mathbb B^{k-1}=\{\bv\in\R^{k-1}:\ \|\bv\|<1\}. \]

    The tangent plane at \(\bmu\) is \(T_{\bmu}=\{\mathbf x\in\R^{k}:\ \bmu^{\top}\mathbf x=0\}\). Let \(\bK\) be a \(k\times(k-1)\) matrix with \(\bK^{\top}\bK=\bI_{k-1}\) whose columns span \(T_{\bmu}\); then \(\bGamma=(\bmu,\bK)\) is orthogonal (Lemma 1). For \(k=3\) we write \(\bK=(\bgamma_2,\bgamma_3)\), its first and second axis: the same orientation parameters as in the Kent density (11).

    Tangent coordinates

    The map

    \[ \psi(\bv)=\bigl(1-\|\bv\|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}\bmu+\bK\bv,\qquad \bv\in\mathbb B^{k-1}, \tag{14} \]

    is a bijection from the ball onto the hemisphere \(H_{\bmu}\), with inverse \(\psi^{-1}(\bu)=\bK^{\top}\bu\) (Lemma 1). The vector \(\bv=\bK^{\top}\bu=(v_2,\dots,v_k)^{\top}\) holds the tangent coordinates of \(\bu\): the coordinates, in the basis \(\bK\), of its orthogonal projection onto \(T_{\bmu}\). If \(\theta\) is the angle between \(\bu\) and \(\bmu\), then \(\|\bv\|=\sin\theta\) and \(\bmu^{\top}\bu=\cos\theta\).

    The Gaussian part

    Let \(\bV_0\sim N_{k-1}(\mathbf 0,\Lambda)\) with \(\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_k)\) and \(\lambda_2\ge\dots\ge\lambda_k>0\), the tangent variances. Let \(\bV\) have the distribution of \(\bV_0\) given \(\|\bV_0\|<1\), and set \(\bU=\psi(\bV)\). Then \(\bU\) has the tangent-normal distribution with mean direction \(\bmu\), axes \(\bK\) and tangent variances \(\Lambda\), written \(\bU\sim\mathrm{TN}(\bmu,\bK,\Lambda)\) (Definition 1). Simulating it follows the definition: draw \(\bV_0\), reject it if \(\|\bV_0\|\ge1\), and apply (14).

    The density

    With \(\varphi_{\Lambda}\) the density of \(N_{k-1}(\mathbf 0,\Lambda)\) and \(\pi_{\Lambda}=P(\|\bV_0\|<1)\), the density of \(\bU\) with respect to the surface measure \(\sigma\) on \(S^{k-1}\) is (Lemma 2)

    \[ g(\bu)=\frac{\varphi_{\Lambda}(\bK^{\top}\bu)\;\bmu^{\top}\bu}{\pi_{\Lambda}},\qquad \bu\in H_{\bmu}, \tag{15} \]

    and \(g(\bu)=0\) outside \(H_{\bmu}\). The factor \(\bmu^{\top}\bu\) is the Jacobian of (14),

    \[ d\bv=(\bmu^{\top}\bu)\,\sigma(d\bu), \tag{16} \]

    which turns the density of the tangent coordinates, with respect to area on the flat plane, into a density on the curved sphere. For \(k=3\), written out,

    \[ g(\bu)=\frac{\bmu^{\top}\bu}{2\pi\sqrt{\lambda_2\lambda_3}\;\pi_{\Lambda}}\, \exp\Bigl\{-\frac{(\bgamma_2^{\top}\bu)^2}{2\lambda_2}-\frac{(\bgamma_3^{\top}\bu)^2}{2\lambda_3}\Bigr\}. \]

    The truncation to the ball removes very little: \(1-\pi_{\Lambda}\le2^{(k-1)/2}\exp\{-1/(4\lambda_2)\}\) by (19), which for the default \(\sqrt{\lambda_2}=0.14\) is below \(10^{-5}\).

    Back to the triangle

    On \(\mathcal O\), squaring undoes the square root: \(\bz=\bu^2\). If \(\bmu\) has positive components, every \(\bu\in\mathcal O\) has a positive part \(u_j\), so \(\bmu^{\top}\bu\ge\mu_ju_j>0\); hence \(\mathcal O\subset H_{\bmu}\), and every composition, including one with zero parts, has positive density. The hemisphere is larger than the orthant, however: points of \(H_{\bmu}\) with a negative coordinate are not square roots of any composition. This is the price the manuscript pays for a normalising constant \(\pi_{\Lambda}\) that depends on \(\Lambda\) only. On this page such points stay on the sphere, drawn in red; they are left out of the triangle, and their probability \(P(\bU\notin\mathcal O)\) is reported.

    The TN distribution has the same location and orientation parameters as the Kent distribution, and its tangent variances correspond one-to-one to the Kent concentration and shape through \(\lambda_m=(\kappa-2\beta_m)^{-1}\) (17). Part 7 compares the two.

    Size and shape: \(\kappa\) and \(\beta\)

    For \(k=3\), with \(\beta_2=\beta\) and \(\beta_3=-\beta\) (12), the matching (17) and its inverse (Section 3.2.2) are

    \[ \lambda_2=\frac{1}{\kappa-2\beta},\qquad \lambda_3=\frac{1}{\kappa+2\beta}, \] \[ \kappa=\frac12\Bigl(\frac1{\lambda_2}+\frac1{\lambda_3}\Bigr),\qquad \beta=\frac12\Bigl(\kappa-\frac1{\lambda_2}\Bigr). \]

    So the two tangent variances can be read as a concentration \(\kappa\), which sets the size of the cloud, and an ovalness \(\beta\), which sets its shape. The Gaussian part has semi-axes proportional to \(\sqrt{\lambda_2}\) and \(\sqrt{\lambda_3}\), so its shape is

    \[ \frac{\text{long semi-axis}}{\text{short semi-axis}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_3}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa+2\beta}{\kappa-2\beta}}, \]

    which depends on \(\beta/\kappa\) only: with \(\kappa=60\) and \(\beta=24\) it is \(\sqrt{108/12}=3\). The condition \(0\le2\beta<\kappa\) of (13) is what keeps \(\lambda_2\) positive and finite; the manuscript notes that (13) holds exactly when \(\lambda_2\ge\lambda_3>0\).

    Two things differ from the Kent picture. The Gaussian part is cut at the edge of the ball, \(\|\bv\|=1\), so the support ends at the edge of the hemisphere \(H_{\bmu}\). And the contours of \(g\) are the Gaussian ellipses only up to the Jacobian factor \(\bmu^{\top}\bu=(1-\|\bv\|^2)^{1/2}\) of (16), which pulls them in and rounds them when the spread is large. Lower \(\kappa\) to see both.

    Part 5

    Contour sets

    A contour set is where the density takes one value. Here are the contours of \(g\) on the tangent plane, on the sphere and on the triangle.

    The density (15) peaks at \(\bu=\bmu\), where both \(\varphi_{\Lambda}(\bK^{\top}\bu)\) and \(\bmu^{\top}\bu\) are largest, so \(g(\bmu)=\varphi_{\Lambda}(\mathbf 0)/\pi_{\Lambda}\). We draw the sets where \(g\) equals a fraction \(f\) of this peak; with five levels, \(f=0.1,0.3,0.5,0.7,0.9\), as on the Kent pages. In tangent coordinates, by (14)–(16),

    \[ \frac{g\{\psi(\bv)\}}{g(\bmu)}=\exp\Bigl(-\tfrac12\,\bv^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}\bv\Bigr)\,\bigl(1-\|\bv\|^{2}\bigr)^{1/2}. \]

    Without the last factor, the Jacobian, the contours would be the ellipses \(\bv^{\top}\Lambda^{-1}\bv=-2\ln f\) of the Gaussian part (dashed); the ellipse at level \(f\) holds a share \(1-f\) of \(N(\mathbf 0,\Lambda)\). The Jacobian pulls the contours in slightly, visibly only when the spread is large. Along every ray from the centre the ratio falls from 1 to 0 before the edge of the ball, so each contour is a single closed curve. Mapped by \(\psi\) it becomes a contour on the sphere, and squared it becomes a contour on the triangle. The parts outside \(\mathcal O\) are dashed red on the plane and the sphere, and are absent from the triangle.

    Where the contour comes from

    Put the mean direction at the north pole, \(\bmu=(0,0,1)\), and let \(\bgamma_2\) be the \(x\) axis and \(\bgamma_3\) the \(y\) axis. A point \(\bu=(x,y,z)\) of the sphere then has tangent coordinates \(\bv=\bK^{\top}\bu=(x,y)\) and \(\bmu^{\top}\bu=z\), and by (15), on the upper hemisphere \(z>0\),

    \[ \frac{g(\bu)}{g(\bmu)}=z\,\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{x^2}{2\lambda_2}-\frac{y^2}{2\lambda_3}\Bigr\}. \]

    Write \(\lambda_2,\lambda_3\) through \(\kappa,\beta\) by (17), \(1/\lambda_2=\kappa-2\beta\) and \(1/\lambda_3=\kappa+2\beta\). Then

    \[ \frac{x^2}{2\lambda_2}+\frac{y^2}{2\lambda_3}=\frac{\kappa}{2}\,(x^2+y^2)-\beta\,(x^2-y^2), \]

    and on the sphere \(x^2+y^2=1-z^2\). Taking logarithms, the contour at a fraction \(f\) of the peak, \(g(\bu)=f\,g(\bmu)\), is

    \[ \frac{\kappa}{2}\,z^2+\ln z+\beta\,(x^2-y^2)=C,\qquad C=\frac{\kappa}{2}+\ln f. \]

    Set it beside the Kent density (11) for \(k=3\), whose contours are \(\kappa z+\beta(x^2-y^2)=C\) (see the Kent page). The \(\beta\) term is the same; only \(\kappa z\) becomes \(\tfrac{\kappa}{2}z^2+\ln z\), where \(\ln z\) is the logarithm of the Jacobian \(\bmu^{\top}\bu\) of (16). Because \(\tfrac{\kappa}{2}z^2+\ln z\) increases with \(z\), each point \((x,y)\) gives exactly one height \(z\), so the equation, read in \(x,y,z\) alone, is a surface: a sheet across the sphere. With \(\beta=0\) it is a flat horizontal plane, as for Kent (the von Mises–Fisher case), and \(\beta\) bends it into a saddle, down along \(\bgamma_2\) and up along \(\bgamma_3\). It is scaffolding, not a density, and no data lie on it. Where it cuts the sphere is the contour of \(g\): the points that are possible observations and equally likely.

    One difference from Kent: \(\ln z\to-\infty\) as \(z\to0\), so the TN sheet always stays above \(z=0\). It never meets the lower hemisphere, where the TN density is zero, however low the level \(f\) is.

    Part 6

    Density, simulation and estimation

    The same density as colour, a sample drawn from it, and the parameters estimated back from the sample.

    On the sphere the colour is \(g\) of (15). On the triangle it is, by default, \(g(\sqrt{\bz})\): the value of (15) at the square root of the composition, so that colours and contours on the triangle are those of the sphere, carried over by \(\bz=\bu^2\). This is not a density with respect to area on the triangle. The switch density of Z shows that density instead,

    \[ f_Z(\bz)=\frac{g(\sqrt{\bz})}{4\sqrt{z_1z_2z_3}}, \]

    which is not in the manuscript: it adds the Jacobian of the square-root map, \(\sigma(d\bu)=dz_1\,dz_2/(4\sqrt{z_1z_2z_3})\). It is infinite on the edges wherever \(g\) is positive there, and it integrates over the triangle to \(P(\bU\in\mathcal O)\), not to 1, because the mass outside \(\mathcal O\) has no composition.

    Resample draws a new sample of size \(n\) by Definition 1. The sample moves with the sliders, because the same random numbers are reused until you press the button. Points that land outside \(\mathcal O\) appear on the sphere as red crosses; they are not compositions, so the triangle shows only the others.

    Estimation follows Section 3.2.3: the sufficient statistics (21) with \(\hat{\bM}=S_2/n\), the normalised resultant \(\hat\bmu=\bs_1/\|\bs_1\|\) (22), the Householder reflector (26) with \(\bH=(\hat\bmu,\bB)\), the projected second moment \(S_T=\bB^{\top}\hat{\bM}\bB\), its spectral decomposition \(S_T=\bW\diag(\hat\lambda_2,\hat\lambda_3)\bW^{\top}\) (33) with \(\hat\bK=\bB\bW\), and the Kent parameters (34).

    Part 7

    Kent versus tangent-normal

    The TN distribution was built to share the parameters of the Kent distribution. How alike are their shapes?

    For \(k=3\) the Kent density (11) is

    \[ f(\bu)=c(\kappa,\beta)^{-1}\exp\bigl\{\kappa\,\bmu^{\top}\bu+\beta\bigl[(\bgamma_2^{\top}\bu)^2-(\bgamma_3^{\top}\bu)^2\bigr]\bigr\},\qquad \bu\in S^{2}, \]

    positive on the whole sphere, with \(0\le2\beta<\kappa\) by (13). With \(\beta_2=\beta\) and \(\beta_3=-\beta\) (12), the matching (17), \(\lambda_m=(\kappa-2\beta_m)^{-1}\), gives

    \[ \lambda_2=\frac{1}{\kappa-2\beta},\qquad \lambda_3=\frac{1}{\kappa+2\beta}. \]

    The two distributions then have the same location and orientation and the same normal limit as \(\kappa\to\infty\) (Scealy and Welsh, 2011, Theorem 3), so for large \(\kappa\) their contours coincide. Away from that limit they differ: the Kent density is positive on the whole sphere, the TN density only on the hemisphere \(H_{\bmu}\). Lower \(\kappa\) towards 2 and watch the outer Kent contours reach the edge of the hemisphere and pass it, while the TN contours stay inside. Both families are drawn at the same fractions of their own peaks.

    Part 8

    Putting it together

    1. A composition is a point of the sphere. The square root sends \(\bz\) to the unit vector \(\bu=\sqrt{\bz}\) in the positive orthant \(\mathcal O\); squaring sends it back, and zeros land on the edges.
    2. A centre gives a flat chart. The mean direction \(\bmu\) and an orthonormal basis \(\bK\) of \(T_{\bmu}\) give tangent coordinates \(\bv=\bK^{\top}\bu\), and \(\psi(\bv)=(1-\|\bv\|^2)^{1/2}\bmu+\bK\bv\) goes back (14).
    3. On that chart the TN distribution is exactly normal. \(\bv\) is \(N(\mathbf 0,\Lambda)\) truncated to the unit ball; on the sphere the density is \(\varphi_{\Lambda}(\bK^{\top}\bu)\,\bmu^{\top}\bu/\pi_{\Lambda}\) (15), where \(\bmu^{\top}\bu\) is the Jacobian (16).
    4. It is the Kent distribution's twin. Same \(\bmu\) and \(\bK\), and \(\lambda_m=(\kappa-2\beta_m)^{-1}\) (17); for large \(\kappa\) the two coincide, and \(1-\pi_{\Lambda}\) is exponentially small (19). For small \(\kappa\) they part, and the TN keeps to its hemisphere.
    5. Its shape is estimated in closed form. Given \(\hat\bmu\), the axes and tangent variances are the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of \(S_T=\bB^{\top}\hat{\bM}\bB\) (33); this is what makes the TN split criterion cheap.
    How the numbers on this page are computed
    • \(\pi_{\Lambda}\) (Lemma 2). For \(k-1=2\), writing \(\bV_0=(\sqrt{\lambda_2}X,\sqrt{\lambda_3}Y)\) with \((X,Y)=R(\cos b,\sin b)\) and \(R^2\sim\chi^2_2\), one has exactly \[ 1-\pi_{\Lambda}=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\exp\Bigl\{-\frac{1}{2\lambda_2\cos^2b+2\lambda_3\sin^2b}\Bigr\}\,db. \] The integrand is smooth and periodic, so the trapezoidal rule converges geometrically; it agrees with Ruben's (1962) series, used in the manuscript, to the accuracy of that series.
    • Contours. Along each direction of the tangent plane, the radius where \(g\{\psi(\bv)\}/g(\bmu)=f\) is found by a safeguarded Newton iteration; the points where a contour crosses an edge of \(\mathcal O\) are located by bisection, so the curves on the triangle end exactly on its edges.
    • \(P(\bU\notin\mathcal O)\). \(\mathcal O\) is convex on the sphere, so each ray of the tangent plane leaves its image once, at a radius found in closed form; the probability is then a one-dimensional integral of the same kind as \(\pi_{\Lambda}\).
    • Simulation follows Definition 1 (normal draws by the polar method from a seeded generator, rejection of \(\|\bV_0\|\ge1\), then (14)). Estimation follows (21), (22), (26), (33) and (34).
    • Kent. The normalising constant is \(c(\kappa,\beta)=2\pi\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\kappa t}I_0\{\beta(1-t^2)\}\,dt\), and the masses on the far hemisphere and outside \(\mathcal O\) are computed by adaptive Simpson quadrature. Kent contours solve a quadratic in \(\cos\theta\) per direction.
    • Not in the manuscript: the frame \((\mathbf l,\mathbf s)\) of design K used to turn the axes by \(\psi\), and the density \(f_Z\) of Part 6.
