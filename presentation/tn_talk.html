Compositional data

Compositional data

A simplex is the convex set of all vectors with non-negative parts that sum to a constant:

\[ \mathcal S^{c}_{k}=\Bigl\{(y_1,\dots,y_k)^{\top}\in\R^{k}\ \Bigm|\ y_i\ge 0,\ \ \sum_{i=1}^{k}y_i=c\Bigr\} \]

It is common to set \(c=1\). We refer to data from this space as compositional data.

k = 3: a triangle; each composition is one point

Before talking about compositional data, we first need to understand what the simplex space is. The simplex space is a convex set of vectors with non-negative components, where all the components add up to a constant. Usually, we set this constant equal to one. Data that belong to this space are called compositional data.

Compositional data

Compositional data: a practical view

Let \(\mathbf Y=(Y_1,Y_2,\dots,Y_K)\) be a vector with positive parts. The compositional transformation (closure)

\[ \mathbf S=\Bigl(\frac{Y_1}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}Y_i},\ \dots,\ \frac{Y_K}{\sum_{i=1}^{K}Y_i}\Bigr) \]

gives a vector that lies in the simplex.

Lost: the absolute scale. \(\mathbf Y\) cannot be recovered from \(\mathbf S\): \(\mathbf Y\) and \(10\,\mathbf Y\) give the same \(\mathbf S\).

Kept: the relative information. For any two parts \(i\) and \(j\),

\[ \frac{Y_i}{Y_j}=\frac{S_i}{S_j}. \]

We often start with positive amounts. Divide each component by their total to obtain a composition. This removes absolute scale but preserves relative information. In particular, ratios between components remain unchanged.

Random forests for compositional responses

Machine learning: random forest

  • Why machine learning? For compositional responses: many predictors with complex non-linear patterns.
  • Common approaches: model compositions directly, use log-ratio coordinates, or use square-root coordinates on the sphere.
  • Why random forests? They are flexible, and their splitting rules can be easily adapted to respect the simplex structure.
  • The PhD of van der Westhuizen (2024) inspired the idea of using the tree splitting rule to account for compositional data.

Random forests can learn complex relationships between predictors and compositional responses. We can adapt their splitting rule to the response distribution.

Random forests for compositional responses

Random forests framework

  • Given a sample of size \(n\) and \(m\) covariates: \[ \mathbf X\in\R^{n\times m}\ \ \text{(covariate matrix)},\qquad \mathbf Y\in\R^{n\times k}\ \ \text{(response matrix)} \]
  • Each row of \(\mathbf Y\) is a compositional vector: \[ Y_{ij}\ge0\ \ \text{for all } i,j,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{k}Y_{ij}=1 \]
  • Goal: learn a mapping \(\mathbf x\longrightarrow\mathbf y\) that respects the simplex geometry.
  • After training:
    • predict compositional responses for new inputs
    • estimate the conditional distribution of the responses

Each row contains predictors and a compositional response. We want to predict a new composition and its conditional distribution.

Random forests for compositional responses

Random forest: overview

A random forest is an ensemble of decision trees, usually trained on bootstrap samples of the data. Key tuning parameters:

  • Number of trees (\(B\)): more trees improve stability but increase the cost
  • Tree depth (\(d_{\max}\)): controls model complexity and overfitting
  • Minimum node size (\(n_{\min}\)): the minimum number of observations required to split a node
  • Covariates per split (\(m_{\text{try}}\)): the number of randomly selected covariates considered at each split; chosen for the task and tuned with validation

A random forest is an ensemble of decision trees, usually trained on bootstrap samples. Important settings are the number of trees, maximum depth, minimum node size, and covariates considered at each split. Our later comparison uses all training observations.

Node model 1 · on the simplex

The Dirichlet distribution eq. (8)

\[ f(\bz;\boldsymbol\alpha)=\frac{\Gamma(\alpha_0)}{\prod_{j=1}^{k}\Gamma(\alpha_j)}\prod_{j=1}^{k}z_j^{\alpha_j-1},\qquad \alpha_0=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\alpha_j \]

\(k\) positive parameters; \(m_i=\alpha_i/\alpha_0\). With \(\boldsymbol\alpha=\alpha_0\mathbf m\): \(E(\mathbf Z)=\mathbf m\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(\mathbf Z)=\dfrac{\diag(\mathbf m)-\mathbf m\mathbf m^{\top}}{\alpha_0+1}\)  (9).

A limited covariance matrix

  • \(k\) parameters for \(k-1\) free means and \(k(k-1)/2\) covariances: once the mean is fixed, one scalar \(\alpha_0\) sets the whole matrix
  • every off-diagonal covariance is negative, \(-m_im_j/(\alpha_0+1)\); the correlations depend on \(\mathbf m\) only: \(\rho_{ij}=-\sqrt{\tfrac{m_im_j}{(1-m_i)(1-m_j)}}\)
  • no independent parameter for orientation or elongation at a fixed mean

Support: the open simplex, \(z_j>0\) (2): a composition with a zero part is outside it.

150 draws from Dir(α) · contours at 10, 30, 50, 70, 90% of the peak

The Dirichlet distribution has K positive parameters. Its mean has K minus one free components; the remaining degree of freedom controls concentration. Mean component i is alpha i divided by the sum of the alphas. Once the mean is fixed, one concentration parameter determines the entire covariance matrix.

Square-root transformation · Section 2.1

From the simplex to the sphere

show
\[ \bu=\sqrt{\bz}\quad(u_j=\sqrt{z_j}),\qquad \sum_j u_j^2=\sum_j z_j=1 \]

Every composition becomes a unit vector in the positive orthant

\[ \mathcal O=\{\bu\in S^{k-1}:\ u_j\ge 0\}. \]

One-to-one, with inverse \(\bz=\bu^2\).

Zeros are admissible: \(\sqrt0=0\) lands on an edge of \(\mathcal O\), with no replacement.

150 compositions from Dir(10, 6, 4), the Dirichlet slide's default. Drag to rotate.

For three-component compositional data, all observations lie on a triangle in three-dimensional space: their components are non-negative and sum to one. Take each component's square root. The observations now lie on the non-negative part of the unit sphere, because their squared components sum to one. This is one orthant, not the whole sphere. Zeros remain admissible.

Node model 2 · on the sphere

The Kent distribution eqs. (11)–(13)

For \(\bu=\sqrt{\bz}\) on the sphere \(S^{k-1}\) (Kent 1982; for compositions, Scealy and Welsh 2011):

\[ f(\bu)=c(\kappa,\boldsymbol\beta)^{-1}\exp\Bigl\{\kappa\,\bmu^{\top}\bu+\sum_{m=2}^{k-1}\beta_m(\bgamma_m^{\top}\bu)^2-\Bigl(\sum_{m=2}^{k-1}\beta_m\Bigr)(\bgamma_k^{\top}\bu)^2\Bigr\} \tag{11}\]

Three parts (\(k=3\), the sphere \(S^2\)), with \(\beta_2=\beta\), \(\beta_3=-\beta\) (12):

\[ f(\bu)=c(\kappa,\beta)^{-1}\exp\bigl\{\kappa\,\bmu^{\top}\bu+\beta\bigl[(\bgamma_2^{\top}\bu)^2-(\bgamma_3^{\top}\bu)^2\bigr]\bigr\} \]

For \(k=3\): five numbers — \(\bmu\) (two angles), the turn \(\psi\) of the axes around \(\bmu\), \(\kappa\) and \(\beta\).

parametermeaning
\(\bmu\)mean direction: the peak and centre of the cloud
\(\kappa>0\)concentration: larger \(\kappa\), tighter cloud
\(\bgamma_2,\bgamma_3\)major and minor axes, orthogonal to \(\bmu\) and to each other
\(\beta\ge0\)ovalness: stretches the cloud along \(\bgamma_2\), squeezes it along \(\bgamma_3\)
\(2\beta<\kappa\) (13)keeps \(\bmu\) the single peak
\(c(\kappa,\beta)\)normalising constant: an integral over the sphere, no closed form

For three components, standard Kent has five free parameters. Two describe mean direction; one describes orientation around that direction. Kappa controls concentration, and beta controls ovalness. Spread and orientation can vary more freely than under a Dirichlet with fixed mean.

  • No closed-form constant. For \(k=3\), \(\;c(\kappa,\beta)=2\pi\displaystyle\int_{-1}^{1}e^{\kappa t}\,I_0\{\beta(1-t^2)\}\,dt\); for \(k\) parts a \((k-1)\)-dimensional integral.

Node model 3 · the tangent-normal (TN) distribution

A normal distribution on the tangent plane Def. 1, eqs. (14)–(16)

Mean direction \(\bmu\); tangent plane \(T_{\bmu}=\{\mathbf x:\bmu^{\top}\mathbf x=0\}\) with orthonormal basis \(\bK\) (\(\bK^{\top}\bK=\mathbf I\)). Construction:

  1. Down to the tangent plane: \(\bv=\bK^{\top}\bu\), the tangent coordinates (move each point parallel to \(\bmu\)).
  2. A normal distribution on the plane: \(\bV_0\sim N_{k-1}(\mathbf 0,\Lambda)\), \(\Lambda=\diag(\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_k)\), kept inside the unit ball: \(\bV=\bV_0\mid\|\bV_0\|<1\).
  3. Back to the hemisphere: \(\bU=\psi(\bV)\sim\mathrm{TN}(\bmu,\bK,\Lambda)\), with
\[ \psi(\bv)=\bigl(1-\|\bv\|^2\bigr)^{1/2}\bmu+\bK\bv \tag{14}\]
\[ g(\bu)=\frac{\varphi_{\Lambda}(\bK^{\top}\bu)\;\bmu^{\top}\bu}{\pi_{\Lambda}},\quad \bu\in H_{\bmu} \tag{15}\]

\(\bmu^{\top}\bu\) is the Jacobian of step 3: \(d\bv=(\bmu^{\top}\bu)\,\sigma(d\bu)\) (16). Support: \(H_{\bmu}=\{\bu:\bmu^{\top}\bu>0\}\), not just \(\mathcal O\). Back to the triangle: \(\bz=\bu^2\).

We wanted flexible shapes, similar to Kent, with simpler estimation. Start with square-root observations on the sphere. Their tangent coordinates are K transpose u. In these coordinates, use a zero-mean normal distribution conditioned to lie inside the open unit ball. Map back using the formula: the tangent component plus the positive square-root component along mu. This gives a distribution on the hemisphere centred at mu. Its spherical density includes the Jacobian, mu transpose u. We call it tangent normal because we use a normal distribution in tangent coordinates.

Tangent-normal · contour sets

Contour eq. (15)

A contour of the TN density \(g\) (15) is a set where the density is constant:

\[ \bigl\{\bu\in H_{\bmu}:\ g(\bu)=c\bigr\},\qquad c=f\,g(\bmu),\quad 0<f<1 \]

It can take different shapes:

  • \(\beta=0\): a circle around \(\bmu\); \(\beta>0\): an oval, stretched along \(\bgamma_2\)
  • \(\kappa\) sets its size; the level \(f\) picks which contour (small \(f\): a wide one)

Tangent normal ? computation

Additive statistics make split searches fast

\[T(A)=\left(n_A,\ \mathbf s_1(A)=\sum_{t\in A}\bu^{(t)},\ S_2(A)=\sum_{t\in A}\bu^{(t)}\bu^{(t)\top}\right)\]

For disjoint groups:

\[T(A\cup B)=T(A)+T(B).\]

These summaries determine the moment direction, Gaussian profile score and implemented Jacobian approximation.

Sort a covariate once.

Move a candidate threshold through its ordered values.

Add each observation to the left summaries.

Subtract from the parent summaries to get the right summaries.

\[T(R)=T(\Omega)-T(L).\]

The exact Jacobian \(\sum\log(\hat\bmu^{\top}\bu^{(t)})\) is not determined by these summaries. They are not sufficient statistics for the complete TN likelihood with unknown direction.

The important point is that these statistics are additive. We keep the observation count, sum of square-root vectors, and sum of their outer products. For disjoint groups, we combine them simply by adding. We do not need to recalculate everything from the original observations. Sort a covariate and move its threshold through the ordered values. Update the left summaries, then subtract from the parent to obtain the right summaries. This is useful when evaluating many splits. These summaries support our approximate score; the exact Jacobian still needs the individual observations.

Tangent-normal · estimation and simulation · Section 3.2.3

Why TN is easy to use

1. Location by moments

\[\hat\bmu=\bs_1/\|\bs_1\|,\qquad \hat\bM=S_2/n_A.\]

2. Profile out the Gaussian shape

\[\hat\ell_G=-\frac{n_A(k-1)}2(1+\log 2\pi)-\frac{n_A}2\log|S_T|,\quad S_T=\bB^\top\hat\bM\bB.\]

Axes and tangent variances follow from the eigenvectors and eigenvalues of \(S_T\), if needed.

\[|S_T|=|\hat\bM|\,\hat\bmu^\top\hat\bM^{-1}\hat\bmu.\]

Implemented score: \(\hat\ell_G+\widetilde J\). One Cholesky factor computes the log-determinant; the shape parameters need not be fitted explicitly at each threshold.

With \(R=\|\bs_1\|\), \(\bar c=R/n_A\), the implementation uses the delta2 approximation:

\[\widetilde J=n_A\log\bar c-\frac{\hat\bmu^\top S_2\hat\bmu-n_A\bar c^2}{2\bar c^2}.\]

No iterative parameter estimation in this split score. The moment direction is not its full MLE. Exact location MLE generally needs numerical optimisation.

The term \(-n_A\log\pi_\Lambda\) is omitted; the concentration bound controls this approximation. Full truncated TN shape MLE is not the Gaussian covariance estimate.

First, normalise the sum of square-root observations to estimate mean direction. This is a moment estimate, not maximum likelihood. Given this direction, we maximise the Gaussian part over shape analytically. We avoid numerical optimisation over the full parameter vector. One Cholesky factor gives the required log-determinant, without calculating every axis and variance. Then add the second-order Jacobian approximation from the same summaries. We omit the truncation term under sufficient concentration. This implemented score needs no iterative parameter estimation. Full TN maximum likelihood differs: location generally needs numerical optimisation, and retaining truncation changes shape estimation.

Log-ratio approach

Multivariate logistic normal (MLN)

Additive log-ratio coordinates:

\[Y_i=\log\!\left(\frac{X_i}{X_K}\right),\qquad i=1,\ldots,K-1.\]
\[\boldsymbol Y\sim N_{K-1}(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\Sigma).\]

Flexible covariance in real space.

Inverse transformation:

\[X_i=\frac{\exp(Y_i)}{1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\exp(Y_j)},\quad i<K,\]
\[X_K=\frac1{1+\sum_{j=1}^{K-1}\exp(Y_j)}.\]

Standard log-ratios require strictly positive components.

Another common approach is the multivariate logistic normal distribution. Transform the composition using log-ratios, then assume a multivariate normal distribution. The inverse formulas return a composition. Standard log-ratios require strictly positive components.

Zero components

Why zeros favour the square root

Dirichlet: support is the open simplex (2); a zero part lies outside it, so zeros must be replaced first.

Log-ratios: undefined at zero, and highly variable near zero.

Square root: \(\sqrt0=0\) is an ordinary point on the edge of \(\mathcal O\). If \(\bmu\) has positive parts, \(\bmu^{\top}\bu>0\) on \(\mathcal O\), so every composition, zeros included, has positive TN density; the square root stays bounded.

Design K (matched local experiment): a part near zero in 30% of draws — TN-RF had the lowest Aitchison distance at every n.

Sphere density g(√z), displayed on the triangle; not a simplex density or an atom at zero

Square roots are defined at zero, while standard log-ratios are not. With a positive mean direction, zero-containing compositions remain admissible under the spherical TN model. This does not mean a continuous model assigns positive probability to an exact structural zero.

Summary

Four messages

  1. Dirichlet: a useful simplex model with covariance tied to its mean and one concentration parameter.
  2. The square root maps compositions, including zeros, to the non-negative spherical orthant.
  3. TN: flexible tangent covariance and a fast approximate split score, using a moment direction and additive summaries.
  4. Prediction depends on the data structure. MLN and TN offer different coordinates; no method wins universally.

The Dirichlet is useful, but its covariance is restricted. Square roots provide spherical coordinates that admit zeros. TN gives flexible tangent covariance and an efficient approximate split score. Finally, the simulations favour different models under different data structures. There is no universal winner.
