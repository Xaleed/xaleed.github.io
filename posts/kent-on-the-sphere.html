    Part 1

    What is directional data?

    Some numbers measure how much. Other numbers measure which way. These two kinds of number need different distributions.

    Ordinary data live on a straight line

    Think of the height of a person: 1.62 m, 1.78 m, 1.91 m. These numbers sit on a straight line. The line has no end and no beginning. Big numbers are far from small numbers. This is Euclidean data. The normal distribution was built for exactly this.

    Directional data live on a circle or a sphere

    Now think of wind direction. You measure it as an angle: 0° is north, 90° is east, and so on. After 359° you do not get 360° — you come back to 0°. The line has been bent into a closed loop. This is circular data.

    Two examples to feel the difference:

    • On a line, 350 and 10 are very far apart. The distance is 340.
    • On a circle, 350° and 10° are very close. The distance is only 20°. Both are almost north.

    Why the ordinary average fails

    Suppose you record two wind directions: 350° and 10°. Both winds come from almost exactly north. The ordinary average is

    \[ \frac{350 + 10}{2} = 180^\circ , \]

    which is south. The answer is the exact opposite of the truth. The ordinary average is wrong because it does not know that the scale wraps around.

    The correct method is to turn each angle into a point on the circle first, average the points, and then read off the direction:

    \[ \mathbf u_1=(\cos 350^\circ,\sin 350^\circ)=(0.985,-0.174),\qquad \mathbf u_2=(\cos 10^\circ,\sin 10^\circ)=(0.985,\;0.174). \]

    The average of these two points is \((0.985,\,0)\). Its direction is \(0^\circ\): north. Correct.

    From the circle to the sphere

    A direction in a flat world needs one angle. A direction in a three-dimensional world needs two angles — think of longitude and latitude on the Earth. Every possible direction in 3D can be drawn as one point on the surface of a ball. That surface is called the sphere, and that is where the Kent distribution lives.

    The one sentence to keep

    Directional data are data whose size carries no information — only the direction matters. So we force every observation to have length exactly 1, and we model the resulting points on a sphere.

    Why not just use the normal distribution?

    • Wrong support. A normal distribution puts probability everywhere in \(\mathbb R^k\). But our data can only be at distance 1 from the origin. Almost all of the normal's probability sits where no data can ever be.
    • Wrong mean. The average of points on a sphere is usually inside the sphere, not on it. So the "mean" is not a direction at all.
    • No wrap-around. The normal distribution thinks 350° and 10° are far apart, as we just saw.

    So we need distributions that are defined on the sphere itself. The von Mises–Fisher distribution and the Kent distribution are two of them.

    Part 2

    The sphere

    One definition, and then a picture you can turn around with your finger.

    The unit sphere in \(k\) dimensions is written

    \[ S^{k-1}=\bigl\{\mathbf u\in\mathbb R^{k}: \|\mathbf u\|=1\bigr\}. \]

    Read it slowly:

    What the symbols say
    \(\mathbf u\)
    a list of \(k\) numbers, for example \((u_1,u_2,u_3)\).
    \(\mathbb R^{k}\)
    all possible lists of \(k\) real numbers.
    \(\|\mathbf u\|\)
    the length of the vector, \(\sqrt{u_1^2+\cdots+u_k^2}\).
    \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\)
    we keep only the lists whose length is exactly 1.
    \(S^{k-1}\)
    the set of all such lists. The superscript is \(k-1\), not \(k\), because the surface has one dimension less than the space it sits in.

    Why \(k-1\)? Take \(k=3\). A point in \(\mathbb R^3\) needs three numbers. But a point on the sphere needs only two — longitude and latitude — because the third number is forced by \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\). One constraint removes one dimension. So \(S^{2}\) is a two-dimensional surface living inside three-dimensional space.

    A point is a direction

    Stand at the centre of the ball. Point your arm at some point on the surface. Your arm now shows a direction. Every direction hits the surface in exactly one place, and every place on the surface corresponds to exactly one direction. So "point on the sphere" and "direction in space" are two names for the same thing.

    Three examples with \(k=3\):

    pointcheck the lengthwhere it is
    \((0,0,1)\)\(0+0+1=1\) ✓the north pole
    \((1,0,0)\)\(1+0+0=1\) ✓on the equator, facing us
    \((0.5,0.5,0.7071)\)\(0.25+0.25+0.5=1\) ✓north of the equator, between the first two axes

    And a non-example: \((1,1,1)\) is not on the sphere, because \(1+1+1=3\neq1\). To put it on the sphere, divide by its length \(\sqrt3\): \((0.577,0.577,0.577)\). Dividing a vector by its own length is called normalising. We will do it many times.

    Part 3

    Compositions become points on a sphere

    This is the bridge. It is one square root, and it is exact — no approximation anywhere.

    What a composition is

    A composition is a vector of proportions. The parts are never negative, and they add up to one:

    \[ \mathbf z=(z_1,\dots,z_k),\qquad z_j\ge0,\qquad \sum_{j=1}^{k}z_j=1. \]

    Soil texture is a good example: the fractions of sand, silt and clay in a sample. Another: the market share of three brands. Another: the fraction of a budget spent on three categories.

    The set of all such vectors is called the simplex, written \(\Delta^{k-1}\). For \(k=3\) the simplex is a flat triangle sitting in 3D space, with corners at \((1,0,0)\), \((0,1,0)\) and \((0,0,1)\).

    The square-root transformation

    Take the square root of every part:

    \[ \mathbf u=\sqrt{\mathbf z}=\bigl(\sqrt{z_1},\dots,\sqrt{z_k}\bigr). \]

    Work through the example \(\mathbf z=(0.25,\,0.25,\,0.50)\):

    \[ \mathbf u=\bigl(\sqrt{0.25},\ \sqrt{0.25},\ \sqrt{0.50}\bigr)=(0.5,\ 0.5,\ 0.7071). \]

    Now check the length of \(\mathbf u\):

    \[ \|\mathbf u\|^2=0.5^2+0.5^2+0.7071^2=0.25+0.25+0.50=1 . \]

    So \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\), which means \(\mathbf u\) is on the sphere. And notice that this is the third point from the table in Part 2 — it was a composition all along.

    Why it always works

    Squaring undoes the square root: \(u_j^2=z_j\). So \(\sum_j u_j^2=\sum_j z_j=1\), which is exactly the condition \(\|\mathbf u\|=1\). The constraint "the parts sum to one" becomes the constraint "the squares sum to one". A composition automatically becomes a unit vector. Nothing is lost: you get the composition back with \(\mathbf z=\mathbf u^2\), componentwise.

    Which part of the sphere?

    Square roots are never negative, so every coordinate of \(\mathbf u\) satisfies \(u_j\ge0\). This means \(\mathbf u\) can only land in the corner of the sphere where all coordinates are positive. That corner is called the positive orthant of the sphere. For \(k=3\) it is one eighth of the globe — the piece between the three positive axes.

    So the picture is:

    • flat triangle (the simplex)  ⟷  curved eighth of the sphere,
    • one point of the triangle for each point of the curved piece, and back again,
    • the corners of the triangle go to the poles of the three axes; the centre \((\tfrac13,\tfrac13,\tfrac13)\) goes to \((0.577,0.577,0.577)\).

    What does the square root actually do?

    Two things are worth knowing.

    1. It inflates the small parts. Compare \(z=0.01\) and \(z=0.04\). On the simplex they differ by 0.03. After the square root they are 0.1 and 0.2, which differ by 0.1 — more than three times as much. Small proportions get more room. This is usually what we want: the difference between 1% and 4% of a soil being clay is scientifically large, but on a raw scale it looks tiny.

    2. It gives a sensible notion of distance. Once on the sphere, the natural distance between two compositions is the angle between them. This angle is closely related to the classical Hellinger distance between two probability vectors. In short, the geometry you get is not arbitrary — it is the geometry statisticians already use for comparing sets of proportions.

    Compared with log-ratio methods

    The usual alternative in compositional data analysis is a log-ratio transform (alr, clr, ilr). Those need every part to be strictly positive, because \(\log 0=-\infty\). The square root has no such problem: \(z_j=0\) simply maps to \(u_j=0\), a point on the boundary of the orthant. This is why the square-root/sphere route is attractive when zeros occur, which happens constantly in real data.

    Part 4

    Why not just use von Mises–Fisher?

    We need a distribution on the sphere. The simplest good one is von Mises–Fisher. Let us learn it first, then find out what it cannot do.

    The density

    The von Mises–Fisher distribution, written vMF, has density

    \[ f(\mathbf u)=c_k(\kappa)\,\exp\bigl(\kappa\,\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u\bigr),\qquad \mathbf u\in S^{k-1}. \]

    It has only two parameters.

    The two parameters
    \(\boldsymbol\mu\)
    the mean direction. A unit vector, \(\|\boldsymbol\mu\|=1\). This is where the distribution is centred — the peak, the most likely direction.
    \(\kappa\ge0\)
    the concentration. A single positive number saying how tightly the data cluster around \(\boldsymbol\mu\). It is like the reciprocal of a variance: large \(\kappa\) means small spread.
    \(c_k(\kappa)\)
    the normalising constant. It only makes the density integrate to one; it carries no shape information. For \(k=3\) it equals \(\kappa/(4\pi\sinh\kappa)\).

    Reading the exponent

    The only place \(\mathbf u\) appears is in \(\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u\). This is the dot product of two unit vectors, so

    \[ \boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u=\cos\vartheta , \]

    where \(\vartheta\) is the angle between \(\mathbf u\) and \(\boldsymbol\mu\). So the density is

    \[ f(\mathbf u)\propto e^{\kappa\cos\vartheta}. \]

    Everything now follows from this one formula:

    • \(\vartheta=0\) (you are at \(\boldsymbol\mu\)): \(\cos\vartheta=1\), the density is at its largest. So \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is the mode.
    • \(\vartheta=90^\circ\): \(\cos\vartheta=0\), the density equals the uniform level.
    • \(\vartheta=180^\circ\) (the opposite point): \(\cos\vartheta=-1\), the density is at its smallest.
    • \(\kappa=0\): the exponent is 0 everywhere, so the density is flat. vMF with \(\kappa=0\) is the uniform distribution on the sphere.
    • \(\kappa\) large: \(e^{\kappa\cos\vartheta}\) falls away very fast as \(\vartheta\) grows, so the data hug \(\boldsymbol\mu\).

    How much spread, in numbers

    When \(\kappa\) is large, a useful rule of thumb is that the typical angular distance from \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is about \(1/\sqrt\kappa\) radians. So \(\kappa=100\) gives a cloud of radius roughly \(0.1\) radians \(\approx 6^\circ\). We will make this precise in Part 7.

    The limitation: vMF can only make round clouds

    Look again at the contours in Figure 4. They are circles. Always. This is not an accident of the picture; it is forced by the formula.

    Here is the argument in three lines. The density depends on \(\mathbf u\) only through the angle \(\vartheta\). Two points at the same angle from \(\boldsymbol\mu\) therefore get exactly the same density, no matter which side of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) they are on. The set of points at a fixed angle from \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is a circle around \(\boldsymbol\mu\). So the contours are circles.

    The technical name for this property is rotational symmetry about \(\boldsymbol\mu\): if you spin the sphere around the \(\boldsymbol\mu\) axis, the density does not change at all.

    A numerical demonstration

    Let \(\boldsymbol\mu=(0,0,1)\) and \(\kappa=10\). Take two points that are the same distance from \(\boldsymbol\mu\) but in different tangent directions:

    \[ \mathbf u_a=(0.3,\;0,\;0.9539),\qquad \mathbf u_b=(0,\;0.3,\;0.9539). \]

    Both have \(\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u=0.9539\), so both have exponent \(10\times0.9539=9.539\). The vMF density is identical at the two points. vMF has no way to say "the data spread further in the first direction than in the second".

    Why this matters for compositions

    Take soil texture \((\text{sand},\text{silt},\text{clay})\). In many places, sand and clay trade off against each other while silt stays nearly constant. On the sphere that is a cloud which is long in one tangent direction and narrow in the other — an ellipse, not a circle. vMF must pick one number, \(1/\kappa\), to describe both directions. It will either exaggerate the silt variation or understate the sand–clay variation. There is no setting of \(\kappa\) that gets both right.

    That is the gap. The Kent distribution fills it.

    Part 5

    The Kent distribution

    Now we are ready. Kent takes the vMF exponent and adds one extra piece, which bends the circles into ellipses.

    The density

    For \(\mathbf u\in S^{2}\) (the ordinary sphere, \(k=3\)) the Kent density is

    \[ f(\mathbf u)=\frac{1}{c(\kappa,\beta)}\, \exp\Bigl\{\underbrace{\kappa\,\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u}_{\text{the vMF part}} \;+\;\underbrace{\beta\bigl[(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2-(\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\bigr]}_{\text{the new part}}\Bigr\}. \]

    Set \(\beta=0\) and the new part disappears: you are back to vMF exactly. So Kent contains vMF as a special case. It can never fit worse.

    Every term, one at a time

    \(\kappa\,\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u\)  —  the pull toward the centre

    What it is: a number times the cosine of the angle to \(\boldsymbol\mu\).
    Its job: it makes the density large near \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and small far away.
    Statistically: it fixes the location and the overall tightness.
    Geometrically: on its own it would produce circular contours around \(\boldsymbol\mu\).
    If \(\kappa\) grows: the cloud shrinks toward \(\boldsymbol\mu\) in all directions at once.

    \((\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\)  —  how far along the first axis

    \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) is a unit vector perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\mu\). The dot product \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u\) measures how far \(\mathbf u\) has moved away from \(\boldsymbol\mu\) in the \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) direction. It is a coordinate. Squaring it removes the sign, so moving left and moving right count the same. This is why the ellipse is symmetric.

    \((\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\)  —  how far along the second axis

    The same thing, for the other direction. \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) is perpendicular to both \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\).

    \(\beta\bigl[(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2-(\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\bigr]\)  —  the difference

    This is the heart of Kent. Notice the minus sign. The term adds density when \(\mathbf u\) leans toward \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) and subtracts density when \(\mathbf u\) leans toward \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\). So the distribution becomes generous in one direction and stingy in the other. That stretches the cloud into an ellipse.

    Why a difference and not two separate numbers? Because on the sphere \((\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2+(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2+(\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2=1\) always. Adding the same constant to all three squared terms changes nothing except the normalising constant. So only the difference between the two tangent directions is identifiable. Writing the coefficients as \(\beta_2=\beta\) and \(\beta_3=-\beta\), which sum to zero, is exactly the standard way of removing that redundancy.

    \(c(\kappa,\beta)\)  —  the normalising constant

    The number that makes the density integrate to 1 over the sphere. It does not depend on \(\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) at all, because rotating the sphere cannot change a total. For \(k=3\) it is an infinite series of Bessel functions,

    \[ c(\kappa,\beta)=2\pi\sum_{j\ge0}\frac{\Gamma(j+\tfrac12)}{\Gamma(j+1)}\,\beta^{2j}\Bigl(\frac{2}{\kappa}\Bigr)^{2j+\frac12} I_{2j+\frac12}(\kappa), \]

    which a computer evaluates in a few terms. You do not need it to understand the shape, and you do not need it for contours or simulation, because those only use ratios of densities and the constant cancels.

    A numerical example

    Take \(\boldsymbol\mu=(0,0,1)\), \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2=(1,0,0)\), \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3=(0,1,0)\), \(\kappa=10\), \(\beta=3\). Use the same two points as in Part 4:

    \(\mathbf u_a=(0.3,0,0.9539)\)\(\mathbf u_b=(0,0.3,0.9539)\)
    \(\kappa\,\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf u\)\(10\times0.9539=9.539\)\(10\times0.9539=9.539\)
    \((\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\)\(0.3^2=0.09\)\(0^2=0\)
    \((\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\)\(0^2=0\)\(0.3^2=0.09\)
    \(\beta[\,\cdot-\cdot\,]\)\(3(0.09-0)=+0.27\)\(3(0-0.09)=-0.27\)
    total exponent9.8099.269

    The ratio of the two densities is \(e^{9.809-9.269}=e^{0.54}\approx1.72\). The point lying in the \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) direction is 1.72 times more likely than the point lying the same distance away in the \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) direction. Under vMF that ratio would have been exactly 1. That single number is the whole difference between the two models.

    Where Kent sits in the bigger family

    Both vMF and Kent belong to the Fisher–Bingham family, \(f(\mathbf u)\propto\exp(\boldsymbol\theta^{\!\top}\mathbf u+\mathbf u^{\!\top}A\mathbf u)\). You get Kent by choosing \(\boldsymbol\theta=\kappa\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(A=\boldsymbol\Gamma\,\mathrm{diag}(0,\beta,-\beta)\,\boldsymbol\Gamma^{\!\top}\), where \(\boldsymbol\Gamma=[\boldsymbol\mu,\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3]\). The essential restriction is that \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is an eigenvector of \(A\): the linear pull and the axes of the quadratic part point in compatible directions. That is what keeps the density unimodal, symmetric about \(\boldsymbol\mu\), and elliptical.

    Part 6

    Every parameter, one by one

    Five objects: \(\boldsymbol\mu\), \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\), \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\), \(\kappa\), \(\beta\). Two of them are numbers; three are directions.

    \(\boldsymbol\mu\) — the mean direction

    A unit vector. It says where the cloud of data sits on the sphere. It is the mode (the highest point of the density) and also the mean direction (the average of the data, normalised). Change \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and the whole cloud slides to a different place on the globe; its shape does not change.

    \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) — the long axis

    A unit vector perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\mu\). It points along the direction in which the data spread the most. Changing \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) rotates the ellipse around the \(\boldsymbol\mu\) axis, like turning a steering wheel. The cloud stays in the same place and keeps the same shape; only its orientation changes.

    \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) — the short axis

    A unit vector perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and to \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\). The direction of least spread. In three dimensions \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) is not really a free choice: once you know \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\), the only remaining perpendicular direction is \(\pm\boldsymbol\gamma_3\).

    The orthogonality constraints

    \[ \boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\gamma_2=0,\qquad \boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\gamma_3=0,\qquad \boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\gamma_3=0 . \]

    A dot product of two unit vectors is zero exactly when they are at 90°. So these three equations say: all three vectors are mutually perpendicular. They form a right-angled frame, like the corner of a room.

    Why must this be true?

    • \(\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\gamma_2=0\) and \(\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\gamma_3=0\). \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) and \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) describe how the data spread around \(\boldsymbol\mu\). "Around" means sideways, not along. A direction with a component along \(\boldsymbol\mu\) would be partly describing "closer to or further from the centre", which is already the job of \(\kappa\). Perpendicular means the jobs do not overlap.
    • \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\gamma_3=0\). The two axes of an ellipse are always at right angles. If \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) and \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) were at, say, 60°, they would partly measure the same thing and the two numbers \(\lambda_2,\lambda_3\) would not separate cleanly into "most spread" and "least spread".

    Put the three vectors side by side as columns of a matrix:

    \[ \boldsymbol\Gamma=[\,\boldsymbol\mu\;\;\boldsymbol\gamma_2\;\;\boldsymbol\gamma_3\,],\qquad \boldsymbol\Gamma^{\!\top}\boldsymbol\Gamma=I_3 . \]

    A matrix with this property is called orthogonal. It represents a rotation of the coordinate axes. So the three constraints together are just saying: \(\boldsymbol\Gamma\) is a rotation that takes the standard axes to the natural axes of the data.

    \(\kappa\) — concentration

    How tightly the data cluster. Large \(\kappa\) means a small, tight cloud. Small \(\kappa\) means a big, loose cloud. \(\kappa\) alone does not change the shape of the ellipse, only its size.

    \(\beta\) — ovalness

    How much longer the long axis is than the short axis. \(\beta=0\) gives a circle. Larger \(\beta\) gives a more stretched ellipse. \(\beta\) alone does not move the cloud or change its overall location.

    The constraint \(0\le 2\beta<\kappa\)

    This one looks technical but has a clear geometric reason. In Part 7 we will show that the variance of the data along each axis is

    \[ \lambda_2=\frac{1}{\kappa-2\beta}\quad(\text{along }\boldsymbol\gamma_2), \qquad \lambda_3=\frac{1}{\kappa+2\beta}\quad(\text{along }\boldsymbol\gamma_3). \]

    Now read the condition off these formulas:

    • If \(2\beta<\kappa\), then \(\kappa-2\beta>0\) and \(\lambda_2\) is a positive, finite variance. Good.
    • If \(2\beta=\kappa\), then \(\lambda_2=\infty\). The cloud is infinitely long in the \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) direction — it stops being a bump around \(\boldsymbol\mu\).
    • If \(2\beta>\kappa\), then \(\lambda_2\) is negative, which is impossible for a variance. What actually happens is that \(\boldsymbol\mu\) stops being the peak: the density develops two peaks on either side of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\), like a saddle. The model is still a valid density, but it is no longer the "one elliptical bump" that we wanted.
    • \(\beta\ge0\) is a labelling convention, not a real restriction. If \(\beta\) were negative, the roles of \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) and \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) would simply swap. We fix \(\beta\ge0\) so that \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) always means "long axis".

    Two useful consequences:

    \[ \kappa=\frac12\Bigl(\frac1{\lambda_2}+\frac1{\lambda_3}\Bigr),\qquad \beta=\frac14\Bigl(\frac1{\lambda_3}-\frac1{\lambda_2}\Bigr). \]

    These are how we will turn estimated variances into estimated parameters in Part 15. Check the direction of the logic: \(\lambda_2>\lambda_3\) (more spread along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\)) gives \(1/\lambda_3>1/\lambda_2\), hence \(\beta>0\). Consistent.

    The ellipse's semi-axes are proportional to \(\sqrt{\lambda_2}\) and \(\sqrt{\lambda_3}\), so the shape of the ellipse — how oval it is — is measured by

    \[ \frac{\text{long semi-axis}}{\text{short semi-axis}}=\sqrt{\frac{\lambda_2}{\lambda_3}}=\sqrt{\frac{\kappa+2\beta}{\kappa-2\beta}} . \]

    With \(\kappa=60,\beta=24\): the ratio is \(\sqrt{108/12}=\sqrt9=3\). The ellipse is three times longer than it is wide.

    Part 7

    The picture that explains everything

    The sphere, the mean direction, the flat plane that touches it, and the ellipse drawn on that plane.

    What is the tangent plane?

    Put a sphere on a table. The table touches the sphere at exactly one point. That table is the tangent plane at that point. Formally, the tangent plane (or tangent space) at \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is

    \[ T_{\boldsymbol\mu}=\{\mathbf v\in\mathbb R^{k}:\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top}\mathbf v=0\}, \]

    the set of all directions perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\mu\). For \(k=3\) it is an ordinary flat plane, and it is two-dimensional. In general it has \(k-1\) dimensions — one less than the space, because the direction "straight up along \(\boldsymbol\mu\)" has been removed.

    Why do we care? Because near \(\boldsymbol\mu\), the curved sphere and the flat plane are almost the same. If the data are concentrated (large \(\kappa\)), the cloud occupies only a small patch, and on a small patch the Earth looks flat. This lets us borrow everything we know about the multivariate normal distribution.

    Tangent coordinates

    For an observation \(\mathbf u\), define its tangent part

    \[ \mathbf v=(I-\boldsymbol\mu\boldsymbol\mu^{\!\top})\mathbf u . \]

    In words: take \(\mathbf u\) and throw away the piece that points along \(\boldsymbol\mu\). What is left is the sideways displacement. It lies in the tangent plane. Then the two numbers

    \[ v_2=\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u,\qquad v_3=\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u \]

    are the coordinates of that displacement along the two axes. Notice \(\boldsymbol\gamma_m^{\!\top}\mathbf v=\boldsymbol\gamma_m^{\!\top}\mathbf u\) because \(\boldsymbol\gamma_m\) is perpendicular to \(\boldsymbol\mu\) — so you can use \(\mathbf u\) directly. These are exactly the quantities that appear in the Kent density.

    The key approximation

    When \(\kappa\) is large, write \(\vartheta\) for the angle from \(\boldsymbol\mu\). A second-order Taylor expansion gives \(\cos\vartheta\approx1-\tfrac12(v_2^2+v_3^2)\), so the Kent exponent becomes

    \[ \kappa\cos\vartheta+\beta(v_2^2-v_3^2) \;\approx\; \kappa-\tfrac12\bigl[(\kappa-2\beta)v_2^2+(\kappa+2\beta)v_3^2\bigr]. \]

    Compare this with the exponent of a two-dimensional normal density with independent components, \(-\tfrac12\bigl(v_2^2/\lambda_2+v_3^2/\lambda_3\bigr)\). They match exactly when

    \[ \boxed{\;\lambda_2=\frac1{\kappa-2\beta},\qquad \lambda_3=\frac1{\kappa+2\beta}\;} \]

    So: near its centre, a Kent distribution is a two-dimensional normal distribution drawn on the tangent plane, with variance \(\lambda_2\) along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) and variance \(\lambda_3\) along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\). This is the single most useful fact in the whole subject. It is where the \(\lambda\)'s come from, it explains the constraint on \(\beta\), and it is what makes estimation easy.

    For vMF, set \(\beta=0\): both variances are \(1/\kappa\). Same in every direction. Round. Exactly as we saw in Figure 4.

    Say this out loud until it is automatic

    \(\lambda_2>\lambda_3\) means more spread along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) than along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_3\). The \(\lambda\)'s are variances on the tangent plane. Their square roots are the lengths of the ellipse's semi-axes. And \(\lambda_2>\lambda_3\) is the same statement as \(\beta>0\).

    Part 8

    The second-moment matrix

    One matrix that stores how the data are spread out in every direction at once.

    Definition

    \[ \hat M=\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\mathbf u_i\mathbf u_i^{\!\top}. \]

    Take this apart.

    Reading the formula
    \(\mathbf u_i\)
    the \(i\)-th observation, a unit vector with \(k\) entries. A \(k\times1\) column.
    \(\mathbf u_i^{\!\top}\)
    the same thing written as a row, \(1\times k\).
    \(\mathbf u_i\mathbf u_i^{\!\top}\)
    column times row \(=\) a \(k\times k\) matrix. Its \((j,l)\) entry is \(u_{ij}u_{il}\). This is called an outer product.
    \(\frac1n\sum_i\)
    average these \(n\) matrices, entry by entry.
    \(\hat M\)
    a \(k\times k\) symmetric matrix. Entry \((j,l)\) is the average of \(u_{ij}u_{il}\) over the sample.

    So the diagonal entry \(\hat M_{jj}\) is the average of \(u_{ij}^2\): how much of the data's "energy" lies along coordinate axis \(j\). The off-diagonal entry \(\hat M_{jl}\) is the average of \(u_{ij}u_{il}\): whether coordinates \(j\) and \(l\) tend to be large together.

    Note carefully: this is the raw second moment, not the covariance. We do not subtract a mean. That is on purpose. On a sphere the natural centre is a direction, not a point, and \(\hat M\) handles that naturally.

    Why the trace is always 1

    The trace of a matrix is the sum of its diagonal entries. For our matrix,

    \[ \operatorname{tr}(\hat M)=\sum_{j=1}^{k}\hat M_{jj} =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\sum_{j=1}^{k}u_{ij}^2 =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}\|\mathbf u_i\|^2 =\frac1n\sum_{i=1}^{n}1 =1 . \]

    The reason is simply that every observation has length one. So \(\operatorname{tr}(\hat M)=1\) is not an assumption — it is automatic, and it is a very good arithmetic check on your code. If you compute \(\hat M\) and the trace is not 1, something is wrong.

    A worked example

    Suppose a sample gives

    \[ \hat M=\begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02&0\\0.02&0.03&0\\0&0&0.94\end{pmatrix}. \]

    Trace: \(0.03+0.03+0.94=1.00\). ✓

    What do the numbers say?

    • \(\hat M_{33}=0.94\) is huge. Almost all of the length of every observation is in the third coordinate. So all the data are clustered near \((0,0,\pm1)\) — near the north pole. Since compositions have non-negative entries, it is the north pole: \(\hat\mu\approx(0,0,1)\).
    • \(\hat M_{11}=\hat M_{22}=0.03\) are small. Only a little of the data's energy is sideways. Together they account for \(0.06\), which is the total spread away from the pole.
    • \(\hat M_{12}=0.02\) is not zero. The first and second coordinates move together. When \(u_{i1}\) is positive, \(u_{i2}\) tends to be positive too. So the sideways cloud is tilted — it lies along the diagonal direction \((1,1,0)\), not along either coordinate axis.
    • \(\hat M_{13}=\hat M_{23}=0\). No systematic link between the sideways position and the height. This is what we expect for a symmetric bump centred at the pole.

    A useful identity

    Split the trace into the part along \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and the part in the tangent plane. Because \([\hat{\boldsymbol\mu},\mathbf b_1,\mathbf b_2]\) is an orthogonal frame,

    \[ \underbrace{\operatorname{tr}(\hat M)}_{=1} =\underbrace{\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^{\!\top}\hat M\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}}_{\text{along }\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}} +\underbrace{\operatorname{tr}(S_L)}_{\text{in the tangent plane}} \qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad \operatorname{tr}(S_L)=1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^{\!\top}\hat M\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}. \]

    In the example, \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^{\!\top}\hat M\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=0.94\), so the total tangent spread must be \(1-0.94=0.06\). Remember that number; we are about to compute \(S_L\) and its diagonal will sum to exactly 0.06.

    How do we get \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\)?

    Two equivalent-in-practice recipes:

    1. Normalised sample mean. Compute \(\bar{\mathbf u}=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf u_i\) (this vector is inside the sphere, not on it), then set \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\bar{\mathbf u}/\|\bar{\mathbf u}\|\). The length \(\bar R=\|\bar{\mathbf u}\|\) is between 0 and 1 and is itself informative: \(\bar R\) near 1 means very concentrated data, \(\bar R\) near 0 means scattered data. This is the standard choice for Kent.
    2. Leading eigenvector of \(\hat M\). The direction that captures the most of \(\operatorname{tr}(\hat M)\). This ignores the sign of the data and is the right choice for axial data.

    For concentrated compositional data the two give almost the same answer. We use the first.

    Part 9

    A basis for the tangent plane

    The Householder construction. This is where most confusion lives, so we go slowly.

    The problem

    We have the tangent plane at \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\): a flat, two-dimensional plane floating in three-dimensional space. We want to describe points on it using two numbers, not three. To do that we need two reference directions inside the plane. That pair of directions is called a basis.

    The rulers on a table

    Put two rulers flat on a table, crossing at right angles at some point. Now any spot on the table can be described by two numbers: "40 cm along the first ruler, 15 cm along the second". Those two rulers are a basis for the table.

    Here is the crucial part. You could have put the rulers down at any angle. Rotate both rulers by 30° and they still work perfectly: every spot still gets two numbers. The numbers change, but the spots do not move. The rulers are your choice; the table and whatever is lying on it are not.

    The Householder construction

    We need a rule that, given any \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\), produces two perpendicular unit vectors inside the tangent plane. It must be automatic and it must never break down. The Householder reflection does this.

    A reflection is a mirror. Pick a mirror plane; the reflection sends every point to its mirror image. If \(\mathbf h\) is the direction perpendicular to the mirror, the reflection matrix is

    \[ H=I-\frac{2\,\mathbf h\mathbf h^{\!\top}}{\mathbf h^{\!\top}\mathbf h}. \]

    Now the trick. We want a mirror that sends the first coordinate axis \(\mathbf e_1=(1,0,0)^{\!\top}\) onto \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\). The mirror that does this is the one perpendicular to their difference. So set

    \[ \mathbf h=\mathbf e_1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}. \]

    Then, as a short calculation confirms, \(H\mathbf e_1=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\).

    Why does that help?

    \(H\) is orthogonal, so its three columns are mutually perpendicular unit vectors. Its first column is \(H\mathbf e_1=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\). Therefore the other two columns are automatically perpendicular to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) — which means they lie in the tangent plane. Collect them:

    \[ H=[\,\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\;\;\mathbf b_1\;\;\mathbf b_2\,],\qquad \mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})=[\,\mathbf b_1\;\;\mathbf b_2\,]\ \ (3\times2). \]

    Done. \(\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\) is our pair of rulers. It satisfies \(\mathbf B^{\!\top}\mathbf B=I_2\) (the rulers are unit length and perpendicular) and \(\mathbf B^{\!\top}\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\mathbf 0\) (they lie in the plane).

    Worked numerically for \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=(0,0,1)\)

    \(\mathbf h=\mathbf e_1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=(1,0,-1)\), and \(\mathbf h^{\!\top}\mathbf h=2\). So

    \[ H=I-\mathbf h\mathbf h^{\!\top} =\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix} -\begin{pmatrix}1&0&-1\\0&0&0\\-1&0&1\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0&0&1\\0&1&0\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}. \]

    Column 1 is \((0,0,1)=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\). ✓ So

    \[ \mathbf b_1=(0,1,0),\qquad \mathbf b_2=(1,0,0). \]

    Both are perpendicular to \((0,0,1)\). ✓ Notice the order: the construction gives \(\mathbf b_1=\mathbf e_2\) and \(\mathbf b_2=\mathbf e_1\). It does not give them in the order you might have guessed. That is fine, and it is exactly the point of the next section: the rule is arbitrary, and the arbitrariness does not matter.

    (One edge case: if \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\mathbf e_1\) exactly, then \(\mathbf h=\mathbf 0\) and the formula divides by zero. Software handles this by reflecting onto \(-\mathbf e_1\) instead, or by using \(\mathbf h=\mathbf e_1+\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\). It never happens with real data.)

    The distinction you must not blur

    \(\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\)\(\mathbf K\) (the Kent frame)
    what it istwo arbitrary perpendicular directions in the tangent planethe two principal directions of the data: long axis and short axis
    what it depends ononly \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\), and on a convention (we chose \(\mathbf e_1\))the data — where they actually spread
    the analogywhere you happened to lay the rulersthe shape of the stain on the table
    unique?no — infinitely many valid choicesyes (up to sign), as long as \(\lambda_2\neq\lambda_3\)
    a parameter?no — it is scaffoldingyes — \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) are parameters of the model

    \(\mathbf B\) is a coordinate system. It knows nothing about the data. It is built from \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) alone, before we have looked at how the cloud is shaped. Two different people using two different conventions would get two different \(\mathbf B\)'s. That is allowed. The job of Parts 10–12 is to start from \(\mathbf B\), look at the data, and turn the rulers until they line up with the ellipse. Only then do we have \(\mathbf K\).

    Part 10

    Projecting the second moment onto the plane

    We now express the spread of the data in ruler coordinates. Three-by-three becomes two-by-two.

    The formula

    \[ S_L=\mathbf B^{\!\top}\hat M\,\mathbf B . \]

    Check the sizes: \(\mathbf B^{\!\top}\) is \(2\times3\), \(\hat M\) is \(3\times3\), \(\mathbf B\) is \(3\times2\). The product is \(2\times2\). The subscript \(L\) stands for "local" — local to the tangent plane.

    What it actually computes

    Write \(\mathbf w_i=\mathbf B^{\!\top}\mathbf u_i\) for the two ruler readings of observation \(i\): \(w_{i1}=\mathbf b_1^{\!\top}\mathbf u_i\) and \(w_{i2}=\mathbf b_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u_i\). Then

    \[ S_L=\mathbf B^{\!\top}\Bigl(\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf u_i^{\!\top}\Bigr)\mathbf B =\frac1n\sum_i(\mathbf B^{\!\top}\mathbf u_i)(\mathbf B^{\!\top}\mathbf u_i)^{\!\top} =\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf w_i\mathbf w_i^{\!\top}. \]

    So \(S_L\) is exactly the second-moment matrix of the two-dimensional ruler readings. Nothing mysterious: we flattened each observation onto the table, read off two numbers, and computed their \(2\times2\) second-moment matrix.

    The three entries
    \((S_L)_{11}\)
    the average of \(w_{i1}^2\): how much the data spread along ruler 1.
    \((S_L)_{22}\)
    the average of \(w_{i2}^2\): how much they spread along ruler 2.
    \((S_L)_{12}\)
    the average of \(w_{i1}w_{i2}\): whether the two readings move together. This is a covariance in ruler coordinates.

    The example, continued

    From Part 9, with \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=(0,0,1)\), the Householder basis is \(\mathbf b_1=(0,1,0)\), \(\mathbf b_2=(1,0,0)\). So \(\mathbf B^{\!\top}\hat M\mathbf B\) just picks out the \((2,2),(2,1),(1,2),(1,1)\) entries of \(\hat M\), in that order:

    \[ S_L=\begin{pmatrix}\hat M_{22}&\hat M_{21}\\ \hat M_{12}&\hat M_{11}\end{pmatrix} =\begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02\\0.02&0.03\end{pmatrix}. \]

    Check the trace: \(0.03+0.03=0.06\), which matches \(1-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^{\!\top}\hat M\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=1-0.94=0.06\) from Part 8. ✓

    Why is it not diagonal?

    This is the important question. The off-diagonal entry is \(0.02\), not \(0\). A non-zero off-diagonal entry means: the axes of the data ellipse are not lined up with the rulers.

    Think of it this way. If the cloud were stretched exactly along ruler 1, then whenever \(w_{i1}\) was large, \(w_{i2}\) would be near zero — the product \(w_{i1}w_{i2}\) would be small and would average to zero. A zero off-diagonal entry means "no tilt". Here the entry is positive and about two-thirds the size of the diagonal entries, so the tilt is large. Since \((S_L)_{11}=(S_L)_{22}\), the tilt is exactly 45°.

    And this is not a problem with the data. It is a statement about the rulers: we laid them down at 45° to the ellipse. Turn the rulers by 45° and the off-diagonal entry will vanish. Finding that turn is the job of Part 11.

    Part 11

    Eigen-decomposition

    The machine that finds the right angle to turn the rulers.

    Eigenvectors, without the algebra

    A square matrix takes a vector in and gives a vector out. Usually the output points somewhere different from the input: the matrix both stretches and rotates.

    An eigenvector is a special input direction that does not get rotated. It only gets stretched:

    \[ S\,\mathbf w=\lambda\,\mathbf w . \]

    The number \(\lambda\) is the eigenvalue — the amount of stretch. That is the whole definition: the direction survives, only the length changes.

    For a symmetric matrix (and second-moment matrices are always symmetric) two nice things are guaranteed: the eigenvalues are real, and the eigenvectors are perpendicular to each other. In two dimensions that means: two perpendicular special directions, with one number each.

    What they mean for a second-moment matrix

    When \(S\) is a second-moment (or covariance) matrix, there is a very concrete reading. For any unit direction \(\mathbf a\), the quantity \(\mathbf a^{\!\top}S\,\mathbf a\) is the spread of the data measured along \(\mathbf a\). Ask: which direction gives the biggest value? The answer is the top eigenvector, and the value it gives is the top eigenvalue. Which gives the smallest? The other eigenvector, and its eigenvalue.

    So for us:

    • the eigenvector with the largest eigenvalue is the direction of maximum spread — the long axis of the ellipse;
    • the eigenvalue is the variance along that direction — the square of the semi-axis length.

    Eigen-decomposition is therefore exactly the right tool: we are asking the data "which way are you longest?", and that is precisely the question eigenvectors answer.

    The decomposition

    \[ S_L=W\Lambda W^{\!\top},\qquad W=[\,\mathbf w_2\;\;\mathbf w_3\,],\qquad \Lambda=\begin{pmatrix}\lambda_2&0\\0&\lambda_3\end{pmatrix},\quad\lambda_2\ge\lambda_3 . \]

    Here \(W\) is a \(2\times2\) orthogonal matrix — a rotation of the plane. Read the formula right to left as a recipe: \(W^{\!\top}\) turns the rulers so they line up with the ellipse; \(\Lambda\) stretches by the right amount along each; \(W\) turns back. The numbering starts at 2 to match \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3\); slot 1 belongs to \(\boldsymbol\mu\).

    The example

    For \(S_L=\begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02\\0.02&0.03\end{pmatrix}\) the characteristic equation is \((0.03-\lambda)^2-0.02^2=0\), giving \(0.03-\lambda=\pm0.02\), so

    \[ \lambda_2=0.05,\qquad \lambda_3=0.01 . \]

    Two sanity checks. The trace is preserved: \(0.05+0.01=0.06=0.03+0.03\). ✓ And the determinant: \(0.05\times0.01=0.0005=0.03^2-0.02^2\). ✓

    Now the eigenvectors. For \(\lambda_2=0.05\), solve \(S_L\mathbf w=0.05\,\mathbf w\). Try \(\mathbf w=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\):

    \[ \begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02\\0.02&0.03\end{pmatrix} \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix} =\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0.05\\0.05\end{pmatrix} =0.05\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\1\end{pmatrix}.\quad\text{It works.} \]

    For \(\lambda_3=0.01\), try \(\mathbf w=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\):

    \[ \begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02\\0.02&0.03\end{pmatrix} \tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix} =\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}0.01\\-0.01\end{pmatrix} =0.01\cdot\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1\\-1\end{pmatrix}.\quad\text{It works.} \]

    So

    \[ \mathbf w_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1),\qquad \mathbf w_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1),\qquad W=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}. \]

    Why these directions? \(\mathbf w_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\) means "one unit along ruler 1 and one unit along ruler 2" — the 45° diagonal. That is exactly the tilt we diagnosed from the positive off-diagonal entry in Part 10. The data spread most along that diagonal, with variance \(0.05\). Along the perpendicular diagonal \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\) the variance is only \(0.01\), five times smaller. Five times smaller variance means \(\sqrt5\approx2.24\) times shorter semi-axis.

    From \(\lambda\)'s to Kent parameters

    Use the boxed relations from Part 7 in reverse:

    \[ \hat\kappa=\frac12\Bigl(\frac1{\lambda_2}+\frac1{\lambda_3}\Bigr) =\frac12\Bigl(\frac1{0.05}+\frac1{0.01}\Bigr)=\frac12(20+100)=60, \] \[ \hat\beta=\frac14\Bigl(\frac1{\lambda_3}-\frac1{\lambda_2}\Bigr) =\frac14(100-20)=20 . \]

    Check the constraint: \(2\hat\beta=40<60=\hat\kappa\). ✓ The fit is a legal, unimodal Kent distribution, with an ellipse \(\sqrt{0.05/0.01}=\sqrt5\approx2.24\) times longer than it is wide.

    An honest note on estimation

    The formulas above are the large-concentration moment estimators. They come from the normal approximation on the tangent plane, so they are good when the data are reasonably concentrated — which is the usual situation for compositions. Kent (1982) gives slightly refined moment estimators, and one can also maximise the exact likelihood numerically, which requires the Bessel series for \(c(\kappa,\beta)\). For understanding what the parameters mean, the version above is the right one, and it is what the eigen-decomposition delivers directly.

    Part 12

    How \(\mathbf B\), \(W\) and \(\mathbf K\) fit together

    The single most important equation in the estimation procedure — and the demonstration that it does not depend on your arbitrary choices.

    The equation

    The eigenvectors \(\mathbf w_2,\mathbf w_3\) live in the two-dimensional ruler coordinate system. But \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) must be real directions in three-dimensional space. To convert, multiply by \(\mathbf B\):

    \[ \boxed{\;\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2=\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\,\mathbf w_2,\qquad \hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3=\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\,\mathbf w_3,\qquad\text{that is}\qquad [\,\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3\,]=\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\,W\;} \]

    and the full Kent frame is

    \[ \hat{\mathbf K}=[\,\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\;\;\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3\,] =[\,\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\;\;\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})W\,] = H\begin{pmatrix}1&\mathbf 0^{\!\top}\\ \mathbf 0&W\end{pmatrix}, \]

    where \(H\) is the full \(3\times3\) Householder matrix. The two ways of writing it are the same thing; the second shows clearly that \(\hat{\mathbf K}\) is "the Householder frame, then a rotation inside the tangent plane".

    In words: \(\mathbf B\) says where the plane is; \(W\) says how far to turn inside it; \(\mathbf K\) is the result.

    Check it on the example

    \(\mathbf b_1=(0,1,0)\), \(\mathbf b_2=(1,0,0)\), \(\mathbf w_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1)\). So

    \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2=\mathbf B\mathbf w_2=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl(1\cdot\mathbf b_1+1\cdot\mathbf b_2\bigr) =\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl((0,1,0)+(1,0,0)\bigr)=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0), \] \[ \hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3=\mathbf B\mathbf w_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}\bigl((0,1,0)-(1,0,0)\bigr)=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1,0). \]

    Both are unit vectors, both are perpendicular to \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=(0,0,1)\), and they are perpendicular to each other. ✓ And \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\propto(1,1,0)\) is the diagonal direction that the \(0.02\) off-diagonal entry pointed to all along.

    Why \(\mathbf B\) alone is not \(\mathbf K\)

    Back to the table. \(\mathbf B\) is where you put the rulers. \(\mathbf K\) is the shape of the stain. The rulers were placed before anyone looked at the stain, so of course they do not line up with it. \(W\) is the turn that fixes this — and \(W\) is the only part of the whole procedure that used the data.

    If you skipped \(W\) and reported \(\mathbf B\) as your estimate of \((\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3)\), you would be reporting your own arbitrary convention as if it were a scientific finding. In the example you would claim the long axis is \((0,1,0)\) when it is really \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,1,0)\) — off by 45°.

    The invariance, demonstrated

    Here is the proof that the choice of rulers does not matter. Suppose a second analyst uses rulers turned by an angle \(\theta\):

    \[ \tilde{\mathbf B}=\mathbf B\,R(\theta),\qquad R(\theta)=\begin{pmatrix}\cos\theta&-\sin\theta\\ \sin\theta&\cos\theta\end{pmatrix}. \]

    Their projected second moment is

    \[ \tilde S_L=\tilde{\mathbf B}^{\!\top}\hat M\tilde{\mathbf B} =R(\theta)^{\!\top}\mathbf B^{\!\top}\hat M\mathbf B\,R(\theta) =R(\theta)^{\!\top}S_L\,R(\theta), \]

    which is a different matrix with different entries. Its eigenvalues, however, are unchanged — rotating coordinates cannot change how spread out the data are. And its eigenvector matrix is \(\tilde W=R(\theta)^{\!\top}W\). Therefore

    \[ \tilde{\mathbf B}\tilde W=\mathbf B R(\theta)\,R(\theta)^{\!\top}W=\mathbf B\,W . \]

    The two analysts get exactly the same \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\) and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3\). The arbitrary choice cancels out. This is why the procedure is trustworthy, and it is worth seeing happen.

    Part 13

    Signs and ties

    Two small facts that prevent a lot of confusion when you compare results.

    An eigenvector has no preferred sign

    If \(S\mathbf w=\lambda\mathbf w\), then multiplying both sides by \(-1\) gives \(S(-\mathbf w)=\lambda(-\mathbf w)\). So \(-\mathbf w\) is an eigenvector with the same eigenvalue. The equation cannot tell them apart.

    Geometrically this is obvious: the long axis of an ellipse is a line, not an arrow. Saying "the data spread along \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\)" and "the data spread along \(-\boldsymbol\gamma_2\)" are the same statement, because the ellipse is symmetric about its centre.

    The Kent density agrees. Its exponent contains \((\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\), and \((-\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2=(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\). Flipping the sign leaves the density completely unchanged. So \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) and \(-\boldsymbol\gamma_2\) are not two different models; they are one model with two names. The parameter is not identified beyond its sign, and that is harmless.

    Why it still matters in practice. Software will return whichever sign its algorithm happens to produce. If you fit the same model twice, or compare two groups, or average \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\) across bootstrap samples, random sign flips will ruin the comparison. So fix a convention and apply it every time. Common choices:

    • make the first non-zero component positive;
    • make the largest-in-magnitude component positive;
    • choose the sign that makes \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\) point closest to some fixed reference direction.

    Any of these is fine. Being consistent is what counts. In Part 11 we reported \(\mathbf w_3=\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(1,-1)\); a routine might equally well have returned \(\tfrac1{\sqrt2}(-1,1)\). Same axis.

    One more freedom: \(\boldsymbol\mu\) also has a sign in general directional statistics, but not for us. Compositions map into the positive part of the sphere, so \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) has non-negative entries and \(-\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) is not a possible mean direction. The sign of \(\boldsymbol\mu\) is identified here.

    When the two eigenvalues are equal

    Suppose \(\lambda_2=\lambda_3\). Then every direction in the tangent plane has the same spread. The cloud is round. The "long axis" does not exist, because no axis is longer than any other.

    Mathematically: every vector in the plane is an eigenvector, and \(W\) can be any rotation. The eigen-decomposition is not unique, and a numerical routine will return an essentially arbitrary pair of directions.

    Is this a disaster? No, and here is the reassuring part. \(\lambda_2=\lambda_3\) means

    \[ \hat\beta=\tfrac14\bigl(1/\lambda_3-1/\lambda_2\bigr)=0, \]

    so the fitted model is vMF, and the Kent density does not contain \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2,\boldsymbol\gamma_3\) at all when \(\beta=0\). The directions became unidentifiable exactly at the moment they stopped mattering. The parameters that are meaningless are also invisible.

    The practical warning. Trouble comes from the case where \(\lambda_2\) and \(\lambda_3\) are close but not equal. Then the axes are formally identified, but they are estimated very badly: a tiny change in the data can swing \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\) by a large angle. So before you interpret an estimated axis direction, look at the ratio \(\lambda_2/\lambda_3\). If it is near 1, do not tell a story about the orientation — there is not enough signal to support one. Report \(\hat\beta\) with a confidence interval instead, and if it covers zero, say so.

    Rule of thumb

    An estimated axis direction is worth interpreting when the ellipse is visibly an ellipse. A ratio \(\lambda_2/\lambda_3\) of 2 or more (semi-axis ratio \(\sqrt2\approx1.4\)) is usually enough to see; a ratio of 1.1 is not.

    When is the extra flexibility worth it?

    Use Kent when:

    • Two components of the composition trade off against each other while a third is stable. That is an ellipse, and vMF cannot represent it.
    • You care about the direction of variation, not only its size. \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\) is then a scientific result: "the main way these soils differ is sand against clay".
    • You are computing likelihoods that will be compared — for example the leaf likelihood in a random forest, or a model-selection criterion. A misspecified spread contaminates every such comparison.
    • The correlation structure the data show is not the one the mean predicts. Under vMF, once \(\boldsymbol\pi=\boldsymbol\mu^2\) is fixed, every correlation between parts is fixed too, and all of them are negative.

    Use vMF when:

    • The sample is small. Two extra parameters estimated from 15 observations may cost more in variance than they gain in bias, and \(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\) will be unstable (Part 13).
    • The cloud genuinely looks round — test \(\beta=0\) and find you cannot reject it.
    • You need speed and a closed-form normalising constant in a tight loop.

    Because vMF is the \(\beta=0\) case of Kent, the choice is a nested model comparison. You can fit Kent and test \(H_0:\beta=0\) with a likelihood-ratio test. That is the honest way to decide, rather than arguing about which is "better".

    Part 17

    The whole pipeline

    Seven arrows from raw proportions to a fitted distribution. Here is what each one does.

    composition \(\longrightarrow\) square-root transformation

    Apply \(u_j=\sqrt{z_j}\) to every part. Why: it converts the constraint \(\sum_jz_j=1\) into \(\sum_ju_j^2=1\), which is exactly membership of the unit sphere. It is exact, reversible (\(z_j=u_j^2\)), and it tolerates zeros. After this step, compositional data are directional data.

    square root \(\longrightarrow\) sphere

    The transformed data now lie on the positive orthant of \(S^{k-1}\). Why: this is the step that lets us use the whole toolbox of directional statistics. The simplex and the positive orthant of the sphere hold the same information; the sphere version has a better-behaved geometry.

    sphere \(\longrightarrow\) mean direction

    Compute \(\bar{\mathbf u}=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf u_i\) and set \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}=\bar{\mathbf u}/\|\bar{\mathbf u}\|\). Why: we need a centre before we can describe spread around the centre. Normalising is necessary because \(\bar{\mathbf u}\) itself is inside the sphere, not on it. The discarded length \(\bar R=\|\bar{\mathbf u}\|\) is kept as a concentration diagnostic.

    mean direction \(\longrightarrow\) tangent plane

    Build the Householder basis \(\mathbf B(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu})\), two perpendicular unit vectors spanning \(T_{\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}}\). Why: spread is a \((k-1)\)-dimensional thing living sideways from \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\), and we need coordinates to measure it in. Without a basis we would be trying to describe a two-dimensional shape with three numbers, one of which is redundant.

    tangent plane \(\longrightarrow\) second moment

    Form \(\hat M=\frac1n\sum_i\mathbf u_i\mathbf u_i^{\!\top}\), then \(S_L=\mathbf B^{\!\top}\hat M\mathbf B\). Why: \(\hat M\) records the spread in every direction at once; \(\mathbf B^{\!\top}(\cdot)\mathbf B\) throws away the part along \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) and keeps the sideways part, written in ruler coordinates. This is the "flatten the cloud onto the table" step.

    second moment \(\longrightarrow\) principal directions

    Eigen-decompose, \(S_L=W\Lambda W^{\!\top}\), and lift back with \([\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\;\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3]=\mathbf BW\). Why: eigenvectors answer the question "in which direction is the spread greatest?", which is exactly the definition of \(\boldsymbol\gamma_2\). Multiplying by \(\mathbf B\) converts an answer given in ruler coordinates into a real direction in \(\mathbb R^k\). The arbitrary choice of rulers cancels here.

    principal directions \(\longrightarrow\) Kent distribution

    Convert the eigenvalues into \(\hat\kappa=\tfrac12(1/\lambda_2+1/\lambda_3)\) and \(\hat\beta=\tfrac14(1/\lambda_3-1/\lambda_2)\), and assemble

    \[ \hat f(\mathbf u)\propto\exp\Bigl\{\hat\kappa\,\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^{\!\top}\mathbf u +\hat\beta\bigl[(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2-(\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2\bigr]\Bigr\}. \]

    Why: the tangent-plane normal approximation of Part 7 links variances to \((\kappa,\beta)\), so estimating the variances estimates the parameters. You now have a full probability model: you can compute likelihoods, simulate new compositions (draw \(\mathbf u\), then set \(\mathbf z=\mathbf u^2\)), compare groups, or use the likelihood inside a larger procedure such as a random forest leaf.

    Going back

    Anything you compute on the sphere can be brought back to the simplex by squaring. A fitted mean direction \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) becomes a fitted mean composition \(\hat{\boldsymbol\pi}=\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}^2\). A simulated \(\mathbf u\) becomes a simulated composition \(\mathbf u^2\). A contour on the sphere becomes a region in the ternary diagram. The round trip loses nothing.

    Summary

    Kent distribution in 10 minutes

    Some data are directions, not amounts. A direction is a point on a sphere. Ordinary statistics does not work well there, because the scale wraps around and the average of several points is not itself a direction.

    Compositional data — proportions that add up to one — can be turned into directions. Take the square root of each part. Then the parts still describe the same thing, but now their squares add up to one, which is exactly the rule for being on a sphere. So every composition is a point on the positive corner of a sphere.

    The simplest distribution on a sphere is von Mises–Fisher. It has a centre \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and a tightness \(\kappa\). Its weakness: the cloud of data it produces is always round. The spread is the same in every direction away from the centre. Real compositions often are not like that: two components trade off against each other while a third barely moves, which makes a long, narrow cloud.

    The Kent distribution fixes this. It adds one term, \(\beta[(\boldsymbol\gamma_2^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2-(\boldsymbol\gamma_3^{\!\top}\mathbf u)^2]\), which adds probability in one sideways direction and removes it in the perpendicular one. The cloud becomes an ellipse. Setting \(\beta=0\) gives vMF back, so Kent is the bigger family.

    Near its centre, a Kent distribution is just a two-dimensional normal distribution painted on the flat plane that touches the sphere at \(\boldsymbol\mu\) — the tangent plane. Its variances along the two axes are \(\lambda_2=1/(\kappa-2\beta)\) and \(\lambda_3=1/(\kappa+2\beta)\). That one fact explains everything: why \(\kappa\) controls size, why \(\beta\) controls ovalness, and why we need \(2\beta<\kappa\) (otherwise \(\lambda_2\) would not be a positive variance).

    To fit it: normalise the average of the unit vectors to get \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\). Compute \(\hat M=\frac1n\sum\mathbf u_i\mathbf u_i^{\!\top}\), whose trace is always 1. Build a basis \(\mathbf B\) for the tangent plane with a Householder reflection — two perpendicular "rulers" laid on the plane, chosen by convention, not by the data. Project: \(S_L=\mathbf B^{\!\top}\hat M\mathbf B\). Its off-diagonal entry is usually not zero, which only means the rulers are not lined up with the cloud. Eigen-decompose \(S_L=W\Lambda W^{\!\top}\): the eigenvalues are the two tangent variances and \(W\) is the turn that lines the rulers up. Finally \([\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_2\;\hat{\boldsymbol\gamma}_3]=\mathbf BW\), and \(\hat\kappa,\hat\beta\) come from the eigenvalues.

    The step people find confusing is the last one. \(\mathbf B\) is not \(\mathbf K\). \(\mathbf B\) is where you happened to put your rulers; \(\mathbf K\) is the shape the data actually have. If you change \(\mathbf B\), then \(S_L\) changes and \(W\) changes in the opposite way, and the product \(\mathbf BW\) stays exactly the same. The arbitrary choice cancels.

    What you get at the end is a proper probability model for compositions: a centre, a size, an ovalness, and a direction of maximum variation that you can interpret scientifically — for example, "these soils differ mainly along a sand-versus-clay axis, and hardly at all in silt".

    Summary

    The five most important things to remember

    1. The square root is exact, not an approximation. \(\sum_jz_j=1\) becomes \(\sum_ju_j^2=1\). A composition is a point on the positive part of a sphere, and you can always go back by squaring.
    2. vMF is round; Kent is oval. vMF depends on \(\mathbf u\) only through the angle to \(\boldsymbol\mu\), so it must be the same in every sideways direction. Kent adds one squared term that breaks this, and vMF is the \(\beta=0\) case of Kent.
    3. Near \(\boldsymbol\mu\), Kent is a 2D normal on the tangent plane, with variances \(\lambda_2=1/(\kappa-2\beta)\) and \(\lambda_3=1/(\kappa+2\beta)\). Everything about \(\kappa\), \(\beta\) and the constraint \(2\beta<\kappa\) follows from this.
    4. \(\mathbf B\) is the rulers; \(\mathbf K\) is the stain. \(\mathbf B\) is a convention built from \(\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\) alone and knows nothing about the data. \(W\), from the eigen-decomposition, is the only part that looks at the data. \(\hat{\mathbf K}=[\hat{\boldsymbol\mu}\;\;\mathbf BW]\).
    5. Change the rulers and nothing you report changes. \(\tilde{\mathbf B}=\mathbf BR\) gives \(\tilde S_L=R^{\!\top}S_LR\) and \(\tilde W=R^{\!\top}W\), so \(\tilde{\mathbf B}\tilde W=\mathbf BW\). The eigenvalues are untouched too. Invariance is the reason the method is trustworthy.
