    How the Kent leaf parameters are estimated

    From n compositions in a node to \(\hat\mu,\hat\kappa,\hat\beta,\hat K\) — and to the split gain. Every step is a closed-form formula. There is no optimisation anywhere.

    • \(\hat\mu\) mean direction
    • \(b_2,b_3\) the rulers (B)
    • \(\hat\gamma_2,\hat\gamma_3\) ellipse axes (\(\hat K\))
    • the mirror
    • data
    A note on names

    The current paper draft calls the Householder completion \(\hat K\). On this page we rename it \(\mathbf B\), and we keep \(\hat K\) for the estimate of \(K\), the ellipse axes. So \(\hat K = \mathbf{B}W\): the rulers, then the rotation.

    S0The big picture

    Idea in one sentence

    A node holds \(n\) compositions; we turn them into two moments, and from those two moments we read off every Kent parameter in closed form.

    Each observation is a composition: a list of parts that add up to one. We first put it on a sphere. Then we collect only two summaries of the node: the sum of the vectors, and the sum of their outer products. Everything else is algebra on those two summaries.

    The work splits into two zones. While the tree is searching for a split we need one number per candidate, and we get it cheaply. Only when a node becomes a leaf do we build the full parameter set.

    Why is this step necessary?

    A decision tree evaluates thousands of candidate splits. If each one needed an eigendecomposition the forest would be far too slow. Separating the two zones is what makes the method practical.

    S1From compositions to the sphere

    Idea in one sentence

    Take the square root of each part, and the composition becomes a point on the unit sphere.

    Let \(z=(z_1,\dots,z_k)\) be a composition, so \(z_j\ge 0\) and \(\sum_j z_j = 1\). Put

    $$u_j=\sqrt{z_j},\qquad j=1,\dots,k,$$

    and then renormalise to \(\|u\|=1\). Because \(\sum_j z_j=1\) we already have \(\sum_j u_j^2=1\), so \(u\) lies on the unit sphere \(S^{k-1}\), in the positive orthant (the corner where every coordinate is positive).

    Our example

    \(z=(0.36,\,0.2304,\,0.4096)\) gives \(u=(0.60,\,0.48,\,0.64)\), and \(0.36+0.2304+0.4096=1\).

    Why is this step necessary?

    On the simplex the parts are tied together by \(\sum_j z_j=1\), which is awkward to model. On the sphere the same constraint becomes \(\|u\|=1\), and a whole family of distributions is already built for that.

    S2The Kent distribution and its parameters

    Idea in one sentence

    Kent describes an elliptical cloud on the sphere: a centre, an overall size, a shape, and the directions the ellipse points in.

    The density on the sphere is

    $$f(u)=c(\kappa,\beta)^{-1}\exp\!\Big[\kappa\,\mu^{\mathsf T}u+\sum_{m=2}^{k-1}\beta_m(\gamma_m^{\mathsf T}u)^2-\Big(\sum_{m=2}^{k-1}\beta_m\Big)(\gamma_k^{\mathsf T}u)^2\Big].$$

    In simple words:

    • \(\mu\): the centre of the cloud, the mean direction.
    • \(\kappa\): the overall size. Large \(\kappa\) means a small, tight cloud.
    • \(\beta\): the shape. \(\beta=0\) gives a round cloud, which is the von Mises–Fisher distribution.
    • \(K=(\gamma_2,\dots,\gamma_k)\): where the ellipse axes point.

    Write \(\beta_k:=-\sum_{m=2}^{k-1}\beta_m\), so the \(\beta\)'s sum to zero. The constraint is

    $$\tfrac{\kappa}{2}>\beta_2\ge\dots\ge\beta_{k-1}\ge-(\beta_2+\dots+\beta_{k-1}).$$
    The key message

    \(\beta\) means nothing without \(K\). It says stretch — but stretch in which direction? Only \(K\) answers that. Compare a normal distribution: \(\Sigma=V\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)V^{\mathsf T}\). The eigenvalues correspond to the \(\lambda_m\), and the eigenvectors correspond to \(K\).

    Why is this step necessary?

    Without \(\beta\) and \(K\) the model can only describe round clouds. Real compositional data in a node are usually stretched along one trade-off between components.

    Check yourself

    After estimating \(\hat\mu\), which parameters are still unknown, and what does each describe?

    The directions \(\gamma_2,\gamma_3\) (where the axes point) and the variances \(\lambda_2,\lambda_3\) (how long they are). The variances give \(\kappa\) (size) and \(\beta_2\) (shape).

    S3The key population fact: \(\Gamma\) diagonalises \(M\)

    Idea in one sentence

    In the Kent frame the second-moment matrix is diagonal, so \(\mu\) and the columns of \(K\) are exactly the eigenvectors of \(M\).

    Let \(M=\mathbb E(uu^{\mathsf T})\). In the Kent frame \(u^{*}=\Gamma^{\mathsf T}u\) the exponent of the density is

    $$\kappa u^{*}_1+\sum_{m=2}^{k}\beta_m (u^{*}_m)^2 .$$

    Changing the sign of one tangent coordinate does not change this, because every tangent coordinate appears squared. So \(\mathbb E(u^{*}_i u^{*}_j)=0\) for \(i\neq j\), and therefore

    $$\Gamma^{\mathsf T}M\,\Gamma=\operatorname{diag}\big(\mathbb E(u^{*2}_1),\mathbb E(v_2^2),\dots,\mathbb E(v_k^2)\big)\approx\operatorname{diag}\Big(1-\textstyle\sum_m\lambda_m,\ \lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_k\Big).$$

    Three consequences follow:

    • \(\mu\) is an eigenvector of \(M\);
    • the columns of \(K\) are the other eigenvectors;
    • their eigenvalues are approximately the tangent variances \(\lambda_m\).

    In short: \(M\) contains all the information about direction and spread. The estimator in the rest of this page is simply the sample version of this one fact.

    Why is this step necessary?

    It tells us what to aim for. If the population answer is an eigendecomposition of \(M\), then the sample answer is an eigendecomposition of \(\hat M\). No optimisation is needed.

    S4Sufficient statistics

    Idea in one sentence

    A node needs only three accumulators, and they add up, so a child costs almost nothing to evaluate.

    A sufficient statistic is a summary that carries all the information the model needs; the individual observations can then be forgotten. For a set \(A\) of observations:

    $$n_A=|A|,\qquad s_1(A)=\sum_{t\in A}u^{(t)},\qquad S_2(A)=\sum_{t\in A}u^{(t)}u^{(t)\mathsf T}.$$

    These are additive over disjoint sets. So the right child is the parent minus the left child, and moving one observation from one side to the other costs \(O(k^2)\).

    Our example

    \(\operatorname{tr}S_2(A)=n_A\) always, because every \(u\) has length one. That is a free check that the accumulation is correct.

    Why is this step necessary?

    A split search sweeps a cut point through the sorted values of a covariate. With additive statistics the whole sweep is linear in \(n\), instead of re-reading the data at every cut.

    S5Moment equation 1 (first moment) → \(\hat\mu\)

    Idea in one sentence

    The average of the unit vectors points in the right direction but is too short; normalising it gives \(\hat\mu\).

    In the population, \(\mathbb E(u)=\rho(\kappa,\beta)\,\mu\) with \(\rho=\mathbb E(u^{*}_1)\in(0,1)\). The mean of points on a sphere lies inside the sphere, which is why \(\rho<1\). The moment equation is

    $$\rho\,\mu=\bar u,$$

    and its solution is

    $$\hat\mu=\frac{\bar u}{\|\bar u\|}=\frac{s_1}{\|s_1\|},\qquad \hat\rho=\|\bar u\|.$$
    Our example

    \(s_1=(64.6,\,32.3,\,64.6)\), so \(\|s_1\|=32.3\sqrt{9}=96.9\).
    With \(n=100\): \(\bar u=(0.646,\,0.323,\,0.646)\), \(\hat\rho=0.969\), and

    $$\hat\mu=\left(\tfrac23,\ \tfrac13,\ \tfrac23\right).$$
    Why is this step necessary?

    Everything later is measured relative to \(\hat\mu\). Note that \(\hat\rho\) is not used further: \(\rho(\kappa,\beta)\) has no closed form, so it cannot be inverted to give \(\kappa\) and \(\beta\).

    S6Why the second moment?

    Idea in one sentence

    The mean sees only the centre; the products \(u_iu_j\) are what reveal shape and orientation.

    Two clouds can have the same centre but be stretched in different directions. They then have exactly the same \(\bar u\). Only the second moment \(\hat M=S_2/n\) can tell them apart, because it contains the products \(u_iu_j\).

    Our example

    $$\hat M=\tfrac19\begin{pmatrix}3.99&1.86&3.54\\1.86&1.02&1.86\\3.54&1.86&3.99\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}0.4433&0.2067&0.3933\\0.2067&0.1133&0.2067\\0.3933&0.2067&0.4433\end{pmatrix}$$

    Trace \(=(3.99+1.02+3.99)/9=1\).

    Panels B and C of Figure F3 above are exactly this situation: same centre, same \(\bar u\), different shape and orientation.

    Why is this step necessary?

    Without \(\hat M\) there is no way to estimate \(K\), \(\kappa\) or \(\beta\) at all — only \(\mu\).

    Check yourself

    Which sample moment can tell us the spread and the directions, \(\bar u\) or \(\hat M\), and why?

    \(\hat M\). The mean \(\bar u\) is identical for clouds that share a centre, however differently they are stretched.

    S7Remove the \(\hat\mu\) direction: the projection \(\hat P\)

    Idea in one sentence

    Split every observation into a part along \(\hat\mu\) and a part on the tangent plane, and keep only the second.

    The tangent plane at \(\hat\mu\) is the flat plane that touches the sphere there; think of it as a table resting on the ball. The part of \(u\) along \(\hat\mu\) is \((\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}u)\hat\mu=\hat\mu\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}u\). The part on the table is

    $$\hat P u=(I-\hat\mu\hat\mu^{\mathsf T})u .$$

    \(\hat P\) is the orthogonal projection onto the tangent plane: it drops each point straight down onto the table. Its properties are

    $$\hat P\hat\mu=0,\qquad \hat Px=x \text{ for } x \text{ on the table},\qquad \hat P^{\mathsf T}=\hat P,\qquad \hat P^2=\hat P.$$
    Our example

    For \(u=(0.60,0.48,0.64)\): \(\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}u=0.9867\),
    along \(\hat\mu\): \((0.6578,\,0.3289,\,0.6578)\),
    \(\hat Pu=(-0.0578,\,0.1511,\,-0.0178)\), with \(\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}\hat Pu\approx0\) and \(\|\hat Pu\|\approx0.163\) — small, because \(u\) is near \(\hat\mu\).

    The second moment of the projected data is \(\frac1n\sum(\hat Pu)(\hat Pu)^{\mathsf T}=\hat P\hat M\hat P\), and here

    $$\hat P\hat M\hat P=\tfrac19\begin{pmatrix}0.23&-0.02&-0.22\\-0.02&0.08&-0.02\\-0.22&-0.02&0.23\end{pmatrix}.$$
    Why is this step necessary?

    Two reasons. First, the spread along \(\hat\mu\) is large (0.94) and would hide the small spread on the table (0.06). Second, it carries no new information: because the trace is one, the spread along \(\hat\mu\) equals \(1-(\text{spread on the table})\).

    Check yourself

    Should we keep the spread along \(\hat\mu\)?

    No. It is large and hides the signal, and it carries no new information because the trace is one.

    Write the part of \(u\) on the table.

    \((I-\hat\mu\hat\mu^{\mathsf T})u\).

    S8The problem: \(\hat P\hat M\hat P\) is singular

    Idea in one sentence

    Projecting leaves a \(3\times3\) matrix describing a 2-dimensional table, so one eigenvalue is exactly zero and the determinant dies.

    Since \(\hat P\hat\mu=0\) we have \(\hat P\hat M\hat P\,\hat\mu=0\). So \(\hat\mu\) is an eigenvector with eigenvalue \(0\).

    Our example

    The eigenvalues of \(\hat P\hat M\hat P\) are \(0,\ 0.05,\ 0.01\).

    Then \(\det(\hat P\hat M\hat P)=0\), so \(\ln\det=-\infty\). But the log-likelihood needs \(\ln(\lambda_2\lambda_3)\). Worse, for large \(\kappa\) the real \(\lambda_m\) can be genuinely tiny — say \(10^{-4}\) — and become hard to tell apart from the fake zero.

    Why is this step necessary?

    The cause is simple: three numbers are being used to describe a two-dimensional table. The fix is to describe the table with two numbers, which is what the next sections build.

    Check yourself

    What is \(\hat P\hat M\hat P\,\hat\mu\), and why is that a problem?

    It is \(0\). So one eigenvalue is zero, the determinant is zero, and \(\ln\det=-\infty\).

    S9What we need: rulers on the table

    Idea in one sentence

    Lay two perpendicular unit rulers on the table and measure every point with them; the \(3\times3\) problem becomes a clean \(2\times2\) one.

    The rulers \(b_2,b_3\) must satisfy

    $$b_2^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=b_3^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=0,\qquad b_2^{\mathsf T}b_3=0,\qquad \|b_2\|=\|b_3\|=1 .$$

    In matrix form, with \(\mathbf B=(b_2,\dots,b_k)\):

    $$\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=0,\qquad \mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\mathbf B=I_{k-1}.$$

    Why unit length? A ruler of length 2 would multiply every variance by 4, and the estimated \(\lambda_m\) would be wrong by that factor.

    With such rulers,

    $$S_L=\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\hat M\mathbf B$$

    is \((k-1)\times(k-1)\), has full rank, satisfies \(\det S_L=\prod_m\hat\lambda_m\), and reproduces the projection exactly: \(\hat P\hat M\hat P=\mathbf B\,S_L\,\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\).

    Why is this step necessary?

    It removes the fake zero of S8 without losing anything. The table really is two-dimensional, so two coordinates are enough.

    Check yourself

    Which conditions must the rulers satisfy?

    They must lie on the table, have unit length, and be perpendicular to each other.

    S10The idea: perfect rulers at the north pole

    Idea in one sentence

    At the north pole the rulers are obvious; move the whole rigid frame there with one orthogonal map and the rulers come along for free.

    At \(e_1=(1,0,0)\) the choice is free and obvious: take \(e_2=(0,1,0)\) and \(e_3=(0,0,1)\). Now move the rigid frame \((e_1,e_2,e_3)\) with a single map \(H\) so that \(He_1=\hat\mu\).

    \(H\) must be orthogonal: a matrix with \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\), which is the same as \((Hx)^{\mathsf T}(Hy)=x^{\mathsf T}y\). So it keeps all lengths and all angles. Every ruler condition then holds automatically:

    at the north poleafter applying \(H\)
    \(e_2^{\mathsf T}e_1=0\)\(b_2^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=0\)
    \(e_3^{\mathsf T}e_1=0\)\(b_3^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=0\)
    \(e_2^{\mathsf T}e_3=0\)\(b_2^{\mathsf T}b_3=0\)
    \(\|e_j\|=1\)\(\|b_j\|=1\)
    Why is this step necessary?

    A rotation would work too. But many different rotations send \(e_1\) to \(\hat\mu\), and they are awkward to build in \(k\) dimensions. A mirror is simpler: there is one obvious choice, and it is explicit.

    Check yourself

    Which rulers are free at \(e_1\)? What property must \(H\) have?

    \(e_2\) and \(e_3\). And \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\).

    S11The Householder reflector (the mirror)

    Idea in one sentence

    Stand a mirror halfway between \(e_1\) and \(\hat\mu\); reflecting the frame in it sends \(e_1\) to \(\hat\mu\) and hands you the rulers.

    A Householder reflector is the matrix of a mirror through the origin. Where should the mirror stand? In the middle of the segment from \(e_1\) to \(\hat\mu\), and perpendicular to it. Its normal direction is

    $$v=e_1-\hat\mu .$$

    Because \(\|e_1\|=\|\hat\mu\|=1\) the triangle is isosceles, so that mirror passes through the centre of the sphere. That is what makes \(H\) linear.

    With \(n=v/\|v\|\), a mirror keeps the part lying in the mirror and flips the part along \(n\):

    $$Hx=x-2(n^{\mathsf T}x)n,\qquad H=I-2nn^{\mathsf T}=I-\frac{2vv^{\mathsf T}}{v^{\mathsf T}v}.$$

    Why the factor 2? Subtracting once lands you on the mirror, which is a projection. Subtracting twice carries you through to the other side, which is a reflection.

    Check that it works: \(v^{\mathsf T}e_1=1-\hat\mu_1\) and \(v^{\mathsf T}v=2(1-\hat\mu_1)\), so \(He_1=e_1-v=\hat\mu\). The properties are \(H^{\mathsf T}=H\), \(H^{\mathsf T}H=I\) and \(H^2=I\). Hence

    $$H=(\hat\mu,\,b_2,\dots,b_k)=(\hat\mu,\,\mathbf B),$$

    and the columns have the closed form

    $$b_j=e_j+\hat\mu_j\,\frac{e_1-\hat\mu}{1-\hat\mu_1},\qquad j\ge2 .$$
    Our example

    \(v=\left(\tfrac13,-\tfrac13,-\tfrac23\right)\), \(v^{\mathsf T}v=\tfrac23\), so \(H=I-3vv^{\mathsf T}\):

    $$H=\begin{pmatrix}2/3&1/3&2/3\\1/3&2/3&-2/3\\2/3&-2/3&-1/3\end{pmatrix},\quad \hat\mu=\left(\tfrac23,\tfrac13,\tfrac23\right),\quad b_2=\left(\tfrac13,\tfrac23,-\tfrac23\right),\quad b_3=\left(\tfrac23,-\tfrac23,-\tfrac13\right).$$

    Checks: \(b_2^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=\tfrac29+\tfrac29-\tfrac49=0\); \(b_3^{\mathsf T}\hat\mu=\tfrac49-\tfrac29-\tfrac29=0\); \(b_2^{\mathsf T}b_3=\tfrac29-\tfrac49+\tfrac29=0\).

    Two ways to read the same map

    1. Move the rulers to the data: \(\mathbf B\) is columns \(2\dots k\) of \(H\). 2. Move the data to the north pole: \(\tilde u=Hu\). Then the tangent coordinates are simply components \(2\dots k\) of \(\tilde u\), and \(S_L\) is the lower-right block of \(H\hat MH\).

    This is Kent's (1982) idea: first move the mean to the north pole, then rotate around the pole. Householder does step 1; \(W\) does step 2.

    Why Householder and not something else

    methodproblem
    Gram–Schmidtwhich \(e_j\) do you drop? and it loses orthogonality numerically
    eigenvectors of \(\hat P\hat M\hat P\)exactly the problems of S8
    angle rotationshard to write down for general \(k\)
    Householderexplicit, \(O(k^2)\), numerically stable, deterministic — and it is what QR uses inside
    Check yourself

    Where is the mirror? Write \(H\).

    In the middle of the segment \(e_1\to\hat\mu\), perpendicular to \(v=e_1-\hat\mu\). And \(H=I-2vv^{\mathsf T}/(v^{\mathsf T}v)\).

    S12The tangent block \(S_L\) and the block form of \(\hat M\)

    Idea in one sentence

    In the Householder frame \(\hat M\) breaks into a corner, a cross term and the \(2\times2\) block we actually want.

    $$H\hat MH=\begin{pmatrix}a&c^{\mathsf T}\\ c&S_L\end{pmatrix},\qquad a=\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}\hat M\hat\mu,\qquad c=\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\hat M\hat\mu,\qquad S_L=\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\hat M\mathbf B .$$

    What each block means: \(a\) is the spread along \(\hat\mu\); \(S_L\) is the spread on the table, seen through the rulers; \(c\) is the cross term. In the population \(c=0\), and in a sample it is usually small but not exactly zero — which is why \(\hat\mu\) is in general not an exact eigenvector of \(\hat M\). Also \(a+\operatorname{tr}S_L=1\).

    Our example

    \(a=0.94\), \(c=(0,0)\) (chosen to keep the numbers clean), and

    $$S_L=\begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02\\0.02&0.03\end{pmatrix},\qquad \det S_L=0.0005 .$$

    The ruler coordinates of \(u=(0.60,0.48,0.64)\) are \(\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}u=(0.0933,\,-0.1333)\).

    \(S_L\) is a genuine covariance matrix — a table of the variances and the covariance of the ruler coordinates. The coordinates have mean zero, because \(\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}\bar u=\mathbf B^{\mathsf T}s_1/n=0\) (as \(s_1\) is parallel to \(\hat\mu\)).

    The off-diagonal entry 0.02 gives a correlation of \(0.02/0.03=0.67\). So the cloud is tilted relative to the rulers, and \(b_2,b_3\) are not the ellipse axes. That is no surprise: the mirror never looked at the cloud. Along the true axes the covariance would be zero.

    Check yourself

    Is \(S_L\) diagonal? Are \(b_2,b_3\) the ellipse axes?

    No — the correlation is 0.67. The mirror never looked at the cloud, so its rulers have no reason to line up with the ellipse.

    S13Eigendecomposition: \(\hat\lambda\), \(W\) and \(\hat K=\mathbf BW\)

    Idea in one sentence

    Turn the rulers until the covariance vanishes; the turn is \(W\), and the turned rulers are the ellipse axes.

    An eigendecomposition writes a symmetric matrix as a rotation, a stretch, and the rotation back:

    $$S_L=W\operatorname{diag}(\hat\lambda_2,\hat\lambda_3)W^{\mathsf T}.$$
    Our example

    \(S_L(1,1)^{\mathsf T}=0.05\,(1,1)^{\mathsf T}\) and \(S_L(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}=0.01\,(1,-1)^{\mathsf T}\), so

    $$\hat\lambda_2=0.05,\ w_2=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,1);\qquad \hat\lambda_3=0.01,\ w_3=\tfrac{1}{\sqrt2}(1,-1).$$

    So \(W\) turns the rulers by 45°. Back in \(\mathbb R^3\), \(\hat\gamma_m=\mathbf Bw_m\) and \(\hat K=\mathbf BW\):

    $$\hat\gamma_2=\frac{b_2+b_3}{\sqrt2}=\frac{(1,0,-1)}{\sqrt2},\qquad \hat\gamma_3=\frac{b_2-b_3}{\sqrt2}=\frac{(-1,4,-1)}{3\sqrt2}.$$

    Both have unit length, both are orthogonal to \(\hat\mu\), and they are orthogonal to each other. The full estimated frame is

    $$\hat\Gamma=\begin{pmatrix}2/3&1/\sqrt2&-1/(3\sqrt2)\\ 1/3&0&4/(3\sqrt2)\\ 2/3&-1/\sqrt2&-1/(3\sqrt2)\end{pmatrix}.$$

    The axis coordinates of \(u=(0.60,0.48,0.64)\) are \(\hat K^{\mathsf T}u=(-0.0283,\,0.1603)\). Their squared length, 0.0265, is the same as for the ruler coordinates — a rotation keeps length.

    What the axes mean here. Moving along \(\hat\gamma_2\) sends \(u_1\) up, leaves \(u_2\) fixed and sends \(u_3\) down. Since \(z=u^2\), the main variation in this node is an exchange between component 1 and component 3. The short axis \(\hat\gamma_3\propto(-1,4,-1)\) is "component 2 against 1 and 3 together", which is the rarest change.

    The choice of rulers does not matter

    Use different rulers \(\mathbf BQ\) with \(Q\) orthogonal. Then \(S_L\to Q^{\mathsf T}S_LQ\) and \(W\to Q^{\mathsf T}W\), so \((\mathbf BQ)(Q^{\mathsf T}W)=\mathbf BW\). The answer is unchanged, and so are the eigenvalues.

    Check yourself

    What are the eigenvalues and eigenvectors of \(\begin{pmatrix}0.03&0.02\\0.02&0.03\end{pmatrix}\)?

    0.05 with \((1,1)/\sqrt2\), and 0.01 with \((1,-1)/\sqrt2\).

    What is \(\hat\gamma_2\) in \(\mathbb R^3\)?

    \(\mathbf Bw_2=(1,0,-1)/\sqrt2\).

    S14Moment equation 2 (second moment) → \(K,\kappa,\beta\)

    Idea in one sentence

    Match the projected second moment to its population form; the spectral theorem solves it uniquely, and the eigenvalues convert straight into \(\kappa\) and \(\beta\).

    In the population, \(PMP=K\operatorname{diag}(\mathbb E v_2^2,\dots,\mathbb E v_k^2)K^{\mathsf T}\approx K\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_k)K^{\mathsf T}\). The moment equation is

    $$K\operatorname{diag}(\lambda)K^{\mathsf T}=\hat P\hat M\hat P .$$

    By the spectral theorem this has a unique solution, up to the signs of the columns: that gives \(\hat K\) and \(\hat\lambda\). Then \(1/\lambda_m=\kappa-2\beta_m\); summing over \(m\) and using \(\sum_m\beta_m=0\),

    $$\hat\kappa=\frac{1}{k-1}\sum_m\frac{1}{\hat\lambda_m},\qquad \hat\beta_m=\tfrac12\Big(\hat\kappa-\frac{1}{\hat\lambda_m}\Big).$$
    Our example

    \(1/\hat\lambda_2=20\) and \(1/\hat\lambda_3=100\), so \(\hat\kappa=60\), \(\hat\beta_2=20\), \(\hat\beta_3=-20\).

    Check: \(\hat\lambda_2=1/(60-40)=0.05\) and \(\hat\lambda_3=1/(60+40)=0.01\). The constraint \(30>20\ge-20\ge-20\) holds automatically: ordered \(\hat\lambda\) gives ordered \(\hat\beta\), and \(\hat\lambda_2>0\) forces \(\hat\beta_2<\hat\kappa/2\).

    The counting works out exactly

    First moment: \(k-1\) equations (direction) for \(\mu\), which has \(k-1\) unknowns. Tangent block: \(k(k-1)/2\) equations, for \(K\) given \(\mu\) (\((k-1)(k-2)/2\) unknowns) plus \(\kappa\) and \(\beta\) (\(k-1\) unknowns). And

    $$\frac{(k-1)(k-2)}{2}+(k-1)=\frac{k(k-1)}{2}.$$

    So the model is exactly identified. Closed form, no optimisation.

    The moment information we do not use. \(a\) carries nothing new, because \(a=1-\operatorname{tr}S_L\). And \(c\) gives \(k-1\) further equations; in the population \(c=0\), so \(\mu\) is over-identified — the first moment says \(\mu\propto\bar u\), while the second says \(\mu\) is an eigenvector of \(M\). The estimator uses the first moment, which is the definition of a mean direction and also fixes the sign.

    Exact and approximate parts

    \(\mathbb E(u)=\rho\mu\) and "\(\Gamma^{\mathsf T}M\Gamma\) is diagonal" are both exact, by symmetry. So the equations for \(\mu\) and \(K\) are exact. The link to \((\kappa,\beta)\) is the approximate part: exactly, \(\mathbb E(u^{*}_1)=\partial\ln c/\partial\kappa\) and \(\mathbb E(v_m^2-v_k^2)=\partial\ln c/\partial\beta_m\), but \(c(\kappa,\beta)\) has no closed form. Theorem 1 replaces \(\mathbb E(v_m^2)\) by \(\lambda_m=1/(\kappa-2\beta_m)\), with error \(O(1/\kappa)\).

    For fixed \(\Gamma\) the Kent family is an exponential family, so the ML equations are moment equations too. That is why this moment estimator is close to ML when \(\kappa\) is large.

    Small-sample (REML) adjustment

    \(\hat\lambda_m\leftarrow\hat\lambda_m\cdot n/(n-1)\). With \(n=100\): \(\hat\lambda=(0.050505,\,0.010101)\), giving \(\hat\kappa=59.4\), \(\hat\beta_2=19.8\), \(\hat\beta_3=-19.8\). This changes the reported leaf only, never the split criterion.

    Check yourself

    Solve \(1/(\kappa-2\beta_2)=0.05\) and \(1/(\kappa+2\beta_2)=0.01\).

    \(\kappa=60\) and \(\beta_2=20\).

    S15Why this estimator is a good one

    Idea in one sentence

    It is the sample version of an exact population fact, its variances are proper variances, and given \(\hat\mu\) no other frame fits better.

    1. It is the sample version of S3. The population says \(\Gamma\) diagonalises \(M\); the estimator diagonalises \(\hat M\).
    2. The fitted tangent coordinates have mean zero: \(\sum_t\hat\gamma_m^{\mathsf T}u^{(t)}=\hat\gamma_m^{\mathsf T}s_1=0\). So \(\hat\lambda_m\) really is a variance.
    3. Given \(\hat\mu\) it maximises the Gaussian surrogate. For any frame \(G\) the best variances are \(\operatorname{diag}(G^{\mathsf T}\hat MG)\), and the log-likelihood is \(\text{const}-\frac n2\sum_m\ln\lambda_m\). By Hadamard's inequality, \(\prod_m (G^{\mathsf T}\hat MG)_{mm}\ge\det(G^{\mathsf T}\hat MG)=\det S_L\), with equality only when \(G^{\mathsf T}\hat MG\) is diagonal — that is, when \(G=\hat K\).
    Why is this step necessary?

    It tells us the closed-form answer is not a shortcut that sacrifices quality. It is the best frame under the surrogate we are actually using.

    S16Log-likelihood and split gain (no \(\mathbf B\), no \(W\) needed)

    Idea in one sentence

    The split criterion needs only \(\ln|S_L|\), and a Schur identity gives that from one Cholesky factorisation of \(\hat M\).

    $$\hat\ell(A)=-\frac{n_A(k-1)}{2}\big(1+\ln 2\pi\big)-\frac{n_A}{2}\ln|S_L(A)| .$$

    The split gain is

    $$\Delta\ell=-\tfrac12\Big[n_L\ln|S_L(L)|+n_R\ln|S_L(R)|-n\ln|S_L(\Omega)|\Big],$$

    which rewards children with smaller ellipses.

    The Schur complement identity — the determinant of a block of a matrix, written through the whole matrix — gives

    $$|S_L|=|\hat M|\cdot\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}\hat M^{-1}\hat\mu .$$

    This is exact even when \(c\neq0\), and it does not depend on which completion \(\mathbf B\) we chose. One Cholesky factorisation \(\hat M=LL^{\mathsf T}\) delivers both pieces:

    $$\ln|\hat M|=2\sum_j\ln L_{jj},\qquad \hat\mu^{\mathsf T}\hat M^{-1}\hat\mu=\|L^{-1}\hat\mu\|^2 .$$

    So the Householder reflector and the eigendecomposition are used only at terminal nodes.

    Our example

    \(\ln|S_L|=\ln 0.0005=-7.6009\), so

    $$\hat\ell=-100\,(1+1.8379)-50\,(-7.6009)=-283.79+380.05=96.26 .$$

    Schur check: \(|\hat M|=0.00047\) and \(\hat\mu^{\mathsf T}\hat M^{-1}\hat\mu=1/0.94=1.0638\); their product is \(0.0005\).

    A naming clash worth fixing

    In the paper the matrix is called \(S_L(A)\), and in the split gain \(L\) is also the left child — so \(S_L(L)\) and \(S_L(R)\) read badly. A different name such as \(S_T(A)\) (for tangent) would avoid it.

    Figure F1 at the top shows these two zones side by side.

    S17Model-implied mean

    Idea in one sentence

    Squaring the frame and weighting by the variances returns the mean composition — and it lands on the empirical mean.

    $$\mathbb E(z)=\Gamma^{2}\,\mathbb E(u^{*2}),\qquad \mathbb E(u^{*2})\approx\Big(1-\textstyle\sum_m\lambda_m,\ \lambda_2,\dots,\lambda_k\Big),$$

    where \(\Gamma^2\) means the entrywise square.

    Our example

    \(\mathbb E(u^{*2})\approx(0.94,\,0.05,\,0.01)\), and the squared columns of \(\hat\Gamma\) are \((4/9,1/9,4/9)\), \((1/2,0,1/2)\) and \((1/18,16/18,1/18)\). Hence

    $$\mathbb E(z)\approx(0.4433,\,0.1133,\,0.4433).$$

    Notice that this is exactly \(\operatorname{diag}(\hat M)\). In general the empirical mean of \(z\) is \(\operatorname{diag}(\hat M)\), because \(z_j=u_j^2\); and with the ML divisor, \(1-\sum_m\hat\lambda_m=a\) exactly. So before the REML adjustment and before flooring, the model-implied mean differs from the empirical mean only through the cross term \(c\).

    S18Numerical caveat for Householder

    Idea in one sentence

    If \(\hat\mu\) is already near \(e_1\) the normal \(v\) is nearly zero; flip the sign and the problem disappears.

    If \(\hat\mu\approx e_1\) then \(v^{\mathsf T}v=2(1-\hat\mu_1)\approx0\), and dividing by it causes cancellation. The fix is to use

    $$v=e_1+\hat\mu,$$

    which sends \(e_1\) to \(-\hat\mu\). Columns \(2\dots k\) are still perfectly valid rulers, which is all we need. For compositions \(\hat\mu_1>0\), so then \(v^{\mathsf T}v=2(1+\hat\mu_1)\ge2\) is never small.

    Why is this step necessary?

    It is the difference between a stable implementation and one that silently loses precision in exactly the nodes where the data are most concentrated.
